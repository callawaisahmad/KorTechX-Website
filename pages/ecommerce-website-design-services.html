E-Commerce Website Design Services: What You're Really Paying For

🗓️ Sep 28, 2026⏱️ 16 min read✍️ KorTechX Team
KT
Written by the KorTechX Engineering Team
Engineers and SEO specialists who build hand-coded, high-performance websites, apps and AI automation for growing businesses across the US, Canada, UK, Australia, New Zealand and the UAE.

A design quote is not a price for an image. It is a total for a list of deliverables, and the fastest way to judge ecommerce website design services is to force that list into the open. Two agencies can quote very different numbers and both be honest, because one is selling you a visual refresh and the other is selling you a store that takes payments, tracks stock, survives a busy weekend and still works a year after launch. The number only means something once you know which of those you are buying.

Comparing on price alone fails in this market. The cheapest quote is sometimes exactly right if it is a focused fix to the pages already selling for you, and a trap if it is a full store with nothing specified. The most expensive is not automatically thorough, and the middle quote is often the widest gap of all: real design work on the front, a template under it, no catalogue import, no integrations, and a support window measured in days.

This guide takes the price apart: the deliverables a competent agency should be able to name, how store design differs from brochure design, the shopping-experience details that decide whether traffic converts, the integrations that turn a site into an operating business, the hidden cost of migrating a catalogue, and what support should cost once you are live. If quotes are already on your desk, read the deliverables and cost sections first. If you also want the engineering handled under one roof, see how our web development service covers the build side of a store.

Key takeaways

  • A price is a list of deliverables, not a verdict on taste. Ask for the list in writing with line items, and remember that two quotes differing threefold are usually describing two different projects.
  • Store design is not brochure design. Cart, checkout, payments, inventory and weekly operational work have to be scoped, priced and staffed, and the cheap options become obvious once you do that.
  • Most lost sales come from details nobody demos: a search that returns nothing useful, variants that cannot be selected, a checkout that loses the session, pages that crawl on mobile data.
  • Integrations and catalogue migration are where budgets double. Gateways, carriers, tax, inventory sync, print-on-demand and a clean product import are separate line items, and pretending otherwise is how projects stall.
  • Post-launch support and ownership are not courtesies. Monitoring, updates, backups, small change capacity, code and data ownership belong in the contract with figures attached, before the first invoice.

The deliverables behind the price

A serious e-commerce proposal is a list of named outputs. The ten below are worth holding any provider to. A missing item is not a saving; it is a change request invoiced in month two.

  • User experience audit. What exists today, where shoppers drop, what is slow, what confuses them, and what the numbers say. On a redesign this is frequently the highest-value part of the project.
  • Wireframes and site structure. Low-fidelity layouts for the homepage, category, search results, product, cart and checkout, plus a sitemap and URL plan. Wireframes let you approve structure before anyone falls in love with a colour.
  • Storefront design. Brand, layout, typography, product card design, and every state a shopper can land in: empty search, out of stock, error, success. Custom design costs more than a theme because it is designed around your catalogue and your buyers. Website designing for a shop means deciding those states on purpose rather than accepting the defaults.
  • Secure development. A supported platform or a considered custom stack, clean documented code, HTTPS, sensible permissions and no exposed admin paths. Security is the baseline here, not a premium tier, and it is the same standard you would expect in any serious web development engagement.
  • Integrations. Payments, live shipping rates and labels, tax handling, transactional and abandoned-cart email, and any inventory or ERP sync. Each one is a build item and a testing item.
  • Product upload and catalogue build. Whether it is fifty products or five thousand, someone has to create them, write the descriptions, size the images, set prices, handle variants and check the details.
  • Testing. Real devices, real browsers, and real checkouts including declined cards and interrupted payments. A store that has never been tested end to end is not finished, and testing is the first thing cut when a timeline slips.
  • Training and documentation. Recorded walkthroughs or live sessions for whoever runs the store, plus written notes on editing, discounting, shipping, refunding and exporting.
  • Launch. Staging sign-off, DNS cutover, redirects, SSL, analytics, search console, transactional email proven with a real order, and a plan for the first week live.
  • A support window after launch. A stated period, a named contact, a response expectation and an hourly rate for work beyond it. Anything less means you are maintaining the store alone.

The design entries above are half the invoice. Web development for a store is the storefront frontend, the checkout backend, payment gateway setup, an editable CMS and the integrations above, tested as one system: web development for business that holds up under volume.

How store design differs from brochure design

Brochure sites are read; stores are used. A visitor spends ten seconds judging a brochure site and can leave. A shopper navigates, filters, compares, adds, enters a card, gets interrupted, and comes back expecting to finish. That difference changes the brief, the team, the timeline and the price.

General web design assumes a page has one job. A store has a job at every stage, and a web design agency that has only shipped brochure sites prices it as though the shopper stops at the product page.

FrontBrochure siteOnline store
Product managementFixed pages written once and edited occasionallyHundreds or thousands of structured records with variants, prices, images, tags and stock levels that change daily
Cart and checkoutNot present; the action is a call, a form or a bookingPersistent cart, multi-step checkout, guest and account options, saved addresses, payment authorisation and order confirmation
PaymentsNone, or a single payment linkGateway integration, cards and local wallets, capture, refunds, disputes, and instalment or buy-now-pay-later options where relevant
InventoryNot applicableStock tracking, low and out of stock behaviour, reservations, multi-location counts and channel sync
Growth pathsAdd pages, publish articles, run a campaign landing pageNew categories, bundles, subscriptions, B2B pricing tiers, loyalty, marketplaces and multi-country storefronts
Ongoing operational needsOccasional copy and image editsOrder processing, refunds, shipping exceptions, tax and compliance changes, platform updates and catalogue upkeep every week

Read that as a pricing guide. If a quote for an online store looks like a brochure quote, either the scope is smaller than the store you described, or the operational work has been assumed away.

The shopping-experience details that decide sales

These are the items that rarely appear in a proposal and almost always appear in your conversion rate. Ask to see them working on a phone before you sign anything.

Product discovery and search

Store owners usually judge search by whether a box exists. Shoppers judge it by whether it returns what they typed. Autocomplete, synonyms, typo tolerance, ranking by sales rather than alphabetically, filters that reflect real differences in your catalogue, and a no-results page that suggests something useful are configuration decisions, not luck.

  • Search tested with the queries your customers actually type, including the misspelled ones
  • Filters and sort options on category pages that map to real product differences
  • A no-results page offering alternatives, popular categories and a route to request a product
  • Breadcrumbs, clean URLs and a category tree that matches how people shop

Variants and attributes

Size, colour, material, length, scent, pack size. If a shopper cannot see their options on the product page and select one without losing their place, they leave or buy the wrong thing. Variants also decide whether your stock system can count inventory correctly, which is a data problem before it is a design problem.

  • Variant selectors on the product page, with swatches that carry readable labels
  • Stock shown per variant, and clear handling of combinations that are unavailable
  • A written SKU convention agreed before the catalogue is built rather than after

Cart and checkout UX

Checkout is where design decisions convert or destroy revenue. Guest checkout as the default, no forced account creation, shipping cost revealed early, address autocomplete, inline errors that preserve what the shopper typed, and a payment step that behaves on mobile and on a weak connection.

  • Guest checkout, with account creation offered afterwards rather than used as a gate
  • Shipping and any tax or duty shown early enough to avoid a surprise at the final step
  • Clear validation on the card form and a recoverable response to a failed payment
  • Returning customers get saved details, and confirmation arrives by email immediately

Speed under load

A store is judged hardest when it is busy. Image weight, cache behaviour, search response time and the pile of third-party scripts decide whether a promotion survives contact with real traffic. Oversized imagery and a crowded page of marketing widgets are usually the biggest reasons a store crawls on mobile data. The Core Web Vitals guide from web.dev explains the three measurements behind a fast store, worth checking on a phone rather than office broadband.

  • Compressed, correctly sized images served responsively and in modern formats
  • A minimum of third-party scripts, added deliberately rather than by default
  • Caching and a content delivery network in front of the catalogue
  • Load tested before a campaign rather than discovered during it

Mobile shopping

For most catalogues a large share of sessions start on a phone, and a surprising number of checkouts are finished on one. Comfortable tap targets, a sticky add-to-cart, a checkout keyboard that does not cover the fields and a filter drawer that works with a thumb are small details that account for a large share of revenue.

  • Mobile-first product pages designed for scrolling, not shrunk from a desktop layout
  • Sticky add-to-cart and a checkout form that autofills correctly
  • Thumb-friendly navigation, menus and filter controls

Trust and policy pages

Returns, shipping, privacy, terms, contact and payment security are not legal decoration. They are read at the moment a shopper decides whether to trust you with a card, usually on a phone, in a hurry. If you ship across the US, Canada, UK, Australia, New Zealand and the UAE, those pages should also state plainly which markets you serve, how duties and taxes are handled, and what delivery costs and how long it takes.

  • Returns, refunds and shipping policies written in plain language rather than legal phrasing
  • Privacy and terms presented for the buyer region, with a real contact route behind them
  • Visible payment security signals and a genuine way to reach a person

Integrations that turn a site into a business

Design is the storefront; integrations are the shop floor. A store that takes orders but cannot talk to a carrier, an accountant or a warehouse is an expensive brochure. Each is a real build item with real testing and belongs as its own line in a proposal.

  • Payment gateways. One provider or several, cards plus the methods your buyers actually use, saved cards, recurring billing if you need it, full and partial refunds, and a written plan for failed payments and disputes.
  • Shipping carriers and rates. Live rates, label generation, tracking emails, domestic and international zones, and rules such as free-shipping thresholds. Hand-entered rates are a quiet and reliable way to lose margin.
  • Tax handling. Sales tax, VAT or GST by destination, threshold and exemption rules, and correct receipts. Selling into the US, Canada, UK, Australia, New Zealand and the UAE means several regimes, and manual handling does not scale past a few hundred orders.
  • Email and abandoned cart. Welcome, confirmation, dispatch, delivery, review and cart-recovery flows, with templates your team can edit and sending infrastructure set up properly so your own domain is not blocked.
  • Inventory and ERP sync. Stock, price feeds, purchase orders, supplier lead times and multi-location counts synced with the system you already run. The most common integration bug is the store promising something the warehouse cannot ship.
  • Print-on-demand or fulfilment partners, if you use them. Automated order routing, artwork handling, production and shipping notifications, and a plan for what happens when a partner goes down mid-campaign.

Data migration and importing your catalogue

Catalogue migration is the item most often priced as a rounding error and the one most often delays a launch. It is not a file upload. It is a data job: your products have to arrive with correct titles, descriptions, prices, tax categories, images, variants, stock and tags, and every old URL needs a new destination so search ranking and advertising do not vanish on day one.

  • Bad source data. Missing fields, inconsistent naming, prices in the wrong format, descriptions still carrying tracking tags, and duplicate records that create duplicate pages and split your rankings.
  • Duplicate or inconsistent SKUs. Without a written SKU convention agreed before the import, the same product exists three ways and stock counts become fiction.
  • Image specifications. File size, dimensions, aspect ratio, background, format and ordering all need normalising, or product pages will look broken and load slowly.
  • Redirects and search. Every old product and category URL mapped to a new one. Skipped redirects mean lost rankings, dead links in old emails and paid campaigns pointing at nothing. Start with Google's own SEO starter guide before the build, so titles, descriptions and indexation are settled rather than patched later.
  • Order and customer history. If you are moving platforms, decide early whether history and accounts come with you; it changes the timeline and the compliance position on customer data.

The professional version of this work is unglamorous and reliable: audit and cleanse the data first, agree a field mapping your team signs off, load a trial batch of twenty to fifty products and review them on the real store, then run a staged import with a reconciliation report covering product counts, stock totals and price checks before anything is switched over. Expect it to be quoted separately, and expect it to be worth it. Our ecommerce website development guide covers how migration sits inside the wider build.

Support and maintenance after launch

Launch is a date, not an ending. Platforms update, certificates expire, extensions conflict, a bad deploy breaks checkout at nine at night, and someone needs to add a product or fix a typo next week. Ongoing support is a defined service with a monthly figure, and it belongs in the proposal rather than in an invoice you did not anticipate.

Ongoing web development services cover that decay, and website development services arranged once cost far less than a rebuild triggered by neglect. Ask whether the retainer is fixed and what an extra hour costs.

  • Health checks. Regular checks on uptime, error rates, checkout completion, broken links, certificates and basic speed, so problems are found by monitoring instead of by a customer.
  • Update management. Core, theme and extension updates applied on a tested schedule, with a backup taken first and a rollback plan if an update breaks something.
  • Backups. Automated, off-site, on a schedule that matches how often you change data, with a restore tested rather than assumed.
  • Uptime monitoring. External monitoring with alerts to a real person, including an out-of-hours route for failures that stop checkout.
  • Small feature changes. A monthly allowance for minor work, so a new field, a small layout change or a shipping tweak does not become an hourly invoice.
  • What it typically costs. As a broad guide, hosting and light maintenance for a small store often sits in the low hundreds of dollars a month, while a store needing regular development and active monitoring sits higher. Ask for a breakdown of hosting, licences, monitoring, support hours and development time rather than one number, and ask the rate for urgent work.

How to choose an e-commerce agency

Where ecommerce website design services differ is in the answers, not the portfolios. Put these questions to everyone on your shortlist and note where the replies get vague, because the gaps are the decision.

  • Ask for live stores, not screenshots. Open two or three on a phone and a desktop, then search, add to cart and get to the final checkout step. Judge speed, clarity and how an out-of-stock product is handled, then ask which parts of that build were custom.
  • Check platform experience. Which platforms do they work in weekly, and why is yours the right one? A provider strong on one platform and guessing on another shows it in the details and in the timeline.
  • Ask who uploads the products. Not who designed the import, but who creates your catalogue, writes the descriptions, sets prices and handles variants. This one question reveals whether the price includes a team or just a build slot.
  • Ask about checkout and security in detail. What is the payment flow, what happens on a failed payment or a double submission, who holds the merchant account, how is customer data handled, and what does the compliance picture look like for the markets you sell in.
  • Ask what happens after launch. The support window, response times, the named contact, what is included, what is extra and at what rate. Then ask how you would report a broken checkout at nine at night.
  • Ask about ownership of intellectual property and data. Design files, source code, theme customisations, domains, hosting accounts, product data, customer data and third-party accounts, plus what happens to all of it if you end the relationship. Get it in writing.
  • Ask for line items and a realistic timeline. Deliverables, exclusions, milestones, payment schedule and a go-live date that assumes real feedback from your side. A quote that cannot be compared with another quote is a sales tactic.
  • Check that they understand your markets. Currency, tax, shipping zones, delivery promises, language and spelling for the US, Canada, UK, Australia, New Zealand and the UAE. International selling is exactly where generic providers show their seams.

Ask website development agencies you shortlist whether the people who drew the wireframes write the code, and whether their website development agency services run end to end or arrive one ticket at a time. A web development agency that does both keeps one party accountable when checkout breaks, while a studio and a separate web development agency leave that gap to you.

They are indicative, not quotations: your own numbers move with catalogue size, depth of custom design, number of integrations, content readiness and how much work your own team takes on.

Ask where the timeline actually breaks: the honest answer is nearly always content and feedback, not the build. The bottom line

Ecommerce website design services are a purchase of operating capability, not of decoration. The right scope names its deliverables, handles the shopping experience properly, integrates with the systems that run your business, imports your catalogue without quietly wrecking your data, and arrives with a support arrangement and an ownership agreement attached.

Use this guide as your comparison sheet. Give every provider the same questions, ask for the same line items, and compare the answers instead of the moodboards. You will often find that the cheapest and most expensive quotes are the least interesting ones, and the specific quote is the one worth having.

Frequently asked questions

What is included in e-commerce website design services?+

A complete engagement normally covers a user experience audit, wireframes and site structure, storefront design including empty and error states, secure development, integrations for payments, shipping, tax and email, product upload or catalogue migration, testing across devices and real checkouts, training and documentation, launch with redirects and analytics, and a support window after go-live. Only complexity changes the scope. A smaller store runs on a tighter version of the same list, while a larger catalogue or international selling adds multi-currency, tax regimes, ERP sync and more testing. Ask for the list in writing, because a missing item returns later as an invoice.

How much does e-commerce website maintenance cost per month after launch?+

It depends entirely on what you need, but as a broad guide, hosting plus light maintenance for a small store often sits in the low hundreds of dollars a month, a busier store with content work and monitoring sits higher, and a store needing regular development sits higher again. A useful approach is to ask for the total broken out into hosting, platform and extension licences, monitoring, support hours and any development time, then ask what the rate is for urgent work outside the agreed hours.

Who uploads the products to the store?+

Some quotes include it, some do not, and the difference can be substantial once you count the products. Ask directly: not who designed the import, but who creates the records, writes the descriptions, sizes the images, sets prices, assigns variants and checks the details. A trial batch of twenty to fifty products reviewed on the live store before a full import is the safest way to check quality. You can also load your own catalogue in a structured import file, but the first import is where most data problems surface.

Are e-commerce design services platform independent?+

Website designing principles transfer between industries more easily than platform knowledge does, so a general web design agency can look credible right up until the first platform-specific requirement. Engineering knowledge does not transfer at all: a provider who is strong on one platform and guessing on another will show it in the details, the timeline and the ongoing costs, so ask which platforms they work in weekly and why yours is the right choice. Whichever way you go, insist on three things regardless of platform: you own the design files, source code and customisations, you own your product and customer data and the ability to export it, and the third-party accounts such as domain, hosting and payment gateway sit in your name.

How do I compare two e-commerce design quotes fairly?+

Normalise them before you compare. Take each quote and list the deliverables line by line: templates designed, catalogue size included, who uploads products, which payment and shipping integrations, whether migration is included, testing hours, training, launch tasks, support period and monthly cost. A cheaper quote covering fewer deliverables is not cheaper, it is smaller, and that is the comparison that matters. Then test the two most important claims, usually checkout quality and product handling, by asking to see them on a phone.

Does the service include the web development side of the store?+

Yes, and that is the point. The design engagement and the build are the same project with one team, not two suppliers you have to coordinate yourself. Full web development is included, and the website development services behind it cover the storefront frontend, the checkout backend that holds sessions and stock, payment gateway setup, an editable CMS, and the integrations for shipping, tax, email and inventory, all built and tested before launch. If a quote covers design only, ask who codes it, who owns the result at handover, and who answers when checkout breaks at nine at night.

Do you serve London and USA businesses?+

Yes, and not only those two markets. KorTechX works remotely with clients across London and the wider UK, the USA, Canada, Australia, New Zealand and the UAE, with the same team, the same process and the same scope-based fixed quote wherever you are, so a shortlist of web development agencies in London and web development agencies in the USA is comparing the same process in a different time zone, not a different build, and nothing changes in the deliverables or the phases. What does change by market is tax, currency, shipping zones and delivery expectations, so tell us which countries you sell in during the brief and they are built into the scope.

How is web development cost shown in an e-commerce quote?+

It should be visible rather than buried. The normal structure is a single scope-based fixed quote covering design, build, integrations and store setup, with the development share broken out so you can see what the code costs against the design and the content. As a broad guide, the build is commonly somewhere around half to two-thirds of an initial store quote, though a heavily customised or heavily integrated project can sit further towards the code. If a quote gives you one number with no line items, ask for the split before you compare it with anything else, and check whether the ongoing web development services sit inside that number or arrive as a separate monthly line.
