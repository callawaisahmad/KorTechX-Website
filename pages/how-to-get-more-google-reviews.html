How to Get More Google Reviews (Without Breaking the Rules)

🗓️ Sep 24, 2026⏱️ 29 min read✍️ KorTechX Team

Written by the KorTechX Engineering Team

Here is the quiet truth about Google reviews: they sit at the exact point where your local ranking and your revenue meet. Google weighs review count, rating, and momentum when it decides which businesses earn the prominent spots in the local map pack, and the same few reviews shape the decision of a prospect the moment they land on your profile. Yet most service businesses fail at getting more Google reviews for an unglamorous reason — they ask inconsistently, or they never ask at all. It is rarely that customers will not leave feedback. In most trades a happy client will happily write something; they are simply waiting for a clear, easy invitation at the right moment.

This guide lays out the policy-safe way to get more Google reviews in 2026. You will find the rules you must respect, a simple workflow for a repeatable ask, the right way to handle a bad review, and the handful of numbers worth tracking to tell whether your review activity is actually moving your map pack position and your bottom line.

Key takeaways

  • More reviews improve prominence in the local map pack and build trust with prospects, but only real, earned feedback delivers a lasting benefit.
  • Google's review policies discourage reviews that are incentivised, gated by rating, or simply not genuine, so the safest strategy is to ask every customer, without strings attached.
  • A simple, repeatable ask — delivered at the right moment through your direct review link or a QR card — outperforms complicated review software.
  • A negative review handled warmly and privately can build more trust than a flawless five-star history.
  • Volume and average rating both matter, and steady review velocity signals to Google that your business is active.
  • Tools that automate the ask are fine; tools that buy, fake, or filter reviews risk penalties rather than rankings.
  • Photos, a complete profile, and the Q&A section work together with reviews to lift engagement on your listing.
  • Measure what you do: weekly review count, average rating, and response time, and watch how they line up with map pack shifts.

Why Google reviews matter for local businesses in 2026

It helps to start with a clear-eyed view of what a Google review actually does for your business, because it works on two fronts at once. On the search side, reviews feed into Google's local ranking systems, helping your listing toward the map pack and toward the top of local results for searches like 'plumber near me' or 'roofing company.' On the conversion side, reviews are public proof — visible, verifiable proof — that other customers trusted you enough to put their name next to your business. One asset, two jobs.

Industry research consistently points to reviews as one of the most-cited influences on local search visibility. Google never publishes the complete formula, and the weight of each element shifts from year to year, but the general pattern has been stable for a long time: more reviews, a higher average rating, and steady momentum give you a real edge over competitors who match you in proximity and relevance but trail you badly in reputation.

The map pack is the most contested real estate in local search — the compact block of three businesses that appears above the organic results for a local query. Proximity and relevance put you into the running; reputation is often what decides which nearby business wins the click. When a homeowner sees three plumbers within a mile, the one with thirty honest reviews and a four-and-a-half-star average beats the one with six reviews, even when the map distances are nearly identical. That is the map pack prominence that reviews earn, and it is why the businesses that obsess over review momentum tend to outlast the ones that obsess over everything else.

Reviews as a ranking signal

If your website is the story about your business told in your own words, your reviews are the same story told by your customers — and search engines place real weight on that kind of third-party confirmation. Review count, the recency of new reviews, and the overall star rating all shape how Google weighs your listing against nearby rivals. A steady trickle of fresh reviews also tells the local index that your business is active and current, which matters in a system that is unkind to stale, abandoned profiles. For the full tour of everything that moves the needle in the pack, our guide to ranking on Google Maps in 2026 walks through review signals alongside the rest of the puzzle: categories, services, proximity, and the interaction signals that reinforce them. Reviews are not the only factor, but they are one of the most dependable ones you control directly.

Reviews as a trust signal

The psychology is simple: people trust people more than they trust marketing. A five-star business with a handful of enthusiastic reviews will often beat a four-and-a-half-star business with dozens, because buyers read the words, not just the stars. They look for specifics — 'showed up on time,' 'quoted a fair price,' 'cleaned up after the job' — and those specifics in a customer's own voice are far more persuasive than anything you will write about yourself. Reviews also reduce perceived risk on high-ticket jobs, exactly the kind of decision where a homeowner is nervous about handing money to a stranger they found online. Raw trust is the currency of local service work, and a review profile is its most honest ledger.

Reviews as a conversion asset

Treat the reviews on your profile as a sales page you never wrote. They answer the objections your prospects are silently asking: did they turn up on time, was the price fair, would these customers hire them again? A healthy cluster of recent, specific reviews settles all of that before you ever pick up the phone. The effect carries well beyond the profile itself: reviews feed into click-through rates from the map pack, into the decision to call rather than scroll past, and into the quality of the leads that reach you, because a warmer prospect is more likely to become a paying job. Getting more Google reviews therefore improves both halves of your business at once — it helps you be found, and it helps you be chosen.

The Google review guidelines you must respect

Before you build the machine, understand the rules of the road. Google asks that reviews reflect genuine customer experience — the person must have actually dealt with the business, the content must be on-topic, and it must not be the product of manipulation. Google removes content that is fake, irrelevant, misleading, or sponsored, and businesses that systematically try to game the system can lose the reviews they collected; in repeated or severe cases the whole listing can face suspension. This is a practical risk assessment, not a moral lecture: buy a hundred reviews and you may well lose a hundred genuine ones you had spent years earning.

Read that plainly: the risk is real. That is why every tactic in this article stays strictly inside the guidelines. The practical guidance for requesting reviews is simple enough to fit on a card, and both halves of it matter equally.

  • Do ask every customer for a genuine review, without conditions, rewards, or strings.
  • Do make leaving a review effortless with a direct link or a one-step QR code.
  • Do ask at a natural, positive moment, such as right after a completed job or a paid invoice.
  • Do respond to every review, positive and negative, promptly and professionally.
  • Do follow up gently with customers who said they would write but did not get around to it.
  • Don't offer money, discounts, gifts, or entry into a prize draw in exchange for a review — Google discourages reviews that are incentivised or paid for.
  • Don't filter requests by expected rating. Do not ask only your five-star customers and silently skip anyone who might be lukewarm; skewed review sets can look like manipulation.
  • Don't leave reviews for your own business, or for competitors, and do not ask staff to do it.
  • Don't prime customers — no 'if you loved the job, leave us five stars' phrasing, which reads as a request for controlled, favourable feedback.
  • Don't run review exchanges with other local businesses, where each side reviews the other in return for reciprocity.

What 'gating' really means

That warning deserves its own paragraph. Gating is the practice of steering only satisfied customers toward your review page, usually by asking after the outcome is already clear, so the happy ones flow in and the unhappy ones never get the invitation. The effect is a one-way filter that quietly manufactures a business that looks closer to perfect than real customers would report. Google's policies are built to surface genuine, unbiased feedback, and systematic gating sits outside that design. The safe approach is the unglamorous one: ask everyone, let the occasional negative review stand as the honest cost of doing business, and treat each complaint as a free piece of market research.

The spirit behind the letter

A genuinely honest review program should produce broadly the same result whether your mother, your biggest competitor, or Google itself reads the reviews. If your system is engineered so that only glowing praise ever surfaces, you have not built an asset — you have built a liability.

Keep that test in mind, and most judgement calls start to write themselves.

The mechanics of getting a review: direct links, QR cards, and the aftercare ask

Reviews are rarely the problem — friction is. Your customers intend to review you, and then life happens: the traffic, the next job, the phone buzzing. Your job is to shrink the distance between 'the customer is happy' and 'the review is live' down to a single tap. The mechanics below are the workhorses of a policy-safe review engine, and every one of them is simple on purpose.

Get your direct review link from Google Business Profile

Every Google Business Profile includes a direct 'ask for reviews' link. You will find it under the reviews section of your profile dashboard, and it takes customers straight to a page where they can rate and describe their experience without hunting through search results. Copy that short link somewhere you will always reach it — a pinned note on your phone, a line in your invoicing template, a saved draft — and make it part of the standard finish line of every single job.

Put the link where a customer already is

The link is only as good as its placement. Add it to the footer of your email signature. Add it to the booking confirmation you send before a job arrives. Add it to the final invoice, right under the total, where a satisfied customer is already holding a phone. Add a button inside your aftercare text message, and tuck a copy onto your website's contact and thank-you pages. The principle: a customer should meet your review invitation four or five times across the life of the job, and at least once after they have paid and received the work.

Print the QR card and work it into the point of service

Not every customer wants to type a URL into their phone, and a QR card removes that hurdle entirely. Print a compact card carrying a QR code that resolves straight to your direct review link, and put it where it can be handed over naturally: on the counter after checkout, clipped to the invoice, or in the van at the end of the job. A pleasant mention is all it needs — 'if you were happy with the work, a quick review helps our small crew a lot.' Delivered once, with a smile, it is a welcome invitation. Repeated, demanded, or tied to anything else, it becomes a transaction.

Run an aftercare follow-up workflow

The single most reliable source of reviews is the customer you just finished working for, because your service is front of mind and their satisfaction is still warm. An aftercare workflow captures that moment before it cools. One to three days after the job, send a text or email that does two jobs: first, confirm everything went well and offer help if anything is out of line; second, invite a review through your direct link. Most customers read such a message within the first hour, so timing beats frequency — one well-placed, individual ask beats five automated nagging ones. Automate the timing, and keep the voice yours: write the messages once, in your natural tone, and let them fire at the right intervals.

The ask that stays inside the rules

What you say matters almost as much as when you say it. A genuinely useful ask sounds like this: 'We hope the job went smoothly. If you have a moment, a review of your experience would mean a lot to our small team — here is the link.' Notice what is absent: no five-star framing, no reward, no 'only if you were happy.' Invite the honest record, and take whatever it brings. The short version of this entire section: a direct link, a QR card, and a one-touch aftercare message turn a satisfied customer into a reviewer in about twenty seconds — comfortably inside the attention span of a busy person with a phone in their hand.

Building a repeatable review habit

Strategy does not fail for lack of good ideas; it fails because the good idea is applied once and then forgotten. The businesses with genuinely strong review profiles do not run brilliant campaigns — they run a boring, dependable ritual. The habit has two halves: knowing when to ask, and knowing what to ask through.

When to ask: after success signals

The right moment to ask is not random, and it is not 'whenever the office remembers.' It is tied to clear success signals — moments when the customer has just experienced your best work. The strongest signals are the completion of the job, the payment of the invoice, a positive comment from the customer, or simply a job that ran on time and on budget, start to finish. Ask inside that window, while satisfaction is fresh. For many home services the day after the invoice is paid beats the day of the job itself, because payment is the moment the customer confirms the price was fair and the work was worth it.

A script for the moment

You do not need to be a natural salesperson to ask this well. The tone is friendly and the words are few: 'Thanks again for having us out today. If you have a minute, a quick review of how it went would really help our small business — here is the link.' One sentence, one honest invitation, zero pressure. If the customer hesitates, drop it cheerfully and move on. The review habit survives on repetition and tone, not on push.

Make it part of your weekly routine

Set a small recurring slot — ten quiet minutes, every Friday — to check your review dashboard, look at what arrived, and schedule the week's follow-ups. The habit is the strategy. A business that asks after every job, every week, for a year will out-review a business that runs one glamorous review campaign and then lapses into silence. Handling the negative review without losing your cool

A negative review is not the enemy of your review strategy — silence is. Every business, including the genuinely great ones, collects an occasional complaint, because you cannot deliver on-time, on-budget service to every single customer every single time, and on the odd occasion you will simply be the business a difficult customer decides to vent about. What separates the businesses that keep winning is not their avoidance of negative reviews; it is how they answer.

The response formula that works

  1. Thank them. Open with genuine thanks for taking the time to share, regardless of how unfair the complaint feels.
  2. Acknowledge the specifics. Name the issue they described. If they said the crew arrived late, say you are sorry the crew arrived late. Vague responses read as dismissive.
  3. Correct the record where warranted. If the review contains a clear factual error, the public reply is the place to courteously set the record straight — once, briefly, without arguing.
  4. Move to a private channel. Invite them to call, email, or message you directly so you can put it right. Resolution happens privately; the public thread shows everyone watching that you take complaints seriously.
Thanks for your note, Marie. I am sorry the repair took longer than we quoted — that is not the experience we aim for. We would genuinely like to put this right, so please call the number on your invoice today and ask for Dan, and we will take care of the follow-up ourselves.

That response thanks, acknowledges the specific problem, does not argue, and moves the conversation somewhere useful. It reads to the twenty undecided readers as a business that owns its mistakes and fixes them.

Never argue — seriously, never

The public reply is read by every future customer, not just by the one who complained. A warm, humble answer sways twenty undecided readers for every one it satisfies. An argumentative reply — accusing the customer of lying, demanding that they edit or delete the review, or airing their full history in public — damages your reputation in a way no run of five-star reviews will repair. It also cuts against the spirit of the guidelines: pressuring a reviewer to alter their honest feedback is exactly the kind of manipulation Google discourages. A constructive complaint that outlives your reply is evidence, not damage, that your business cares about getting it right.

Mine the negative reviews for patterns

Beyond the individual response, your negative reviews are free market research with the respondent's name attached. Track the complaint themes in a note or spreadsheet — late arrival, scope confusion, pricing, communication, cleanup. If the same theme surfaces more than twice in a quarter, that is not an unhappy customer; that is a leak in your operations, and the review is telling you exactly where. Fix the leak, and the reviews quietly improve without you having to ask anyone to say anything nicer.

When to report a review

There are legitimate cases to act on. Google provides tools for removing content that breaches its policies: a competitor's fake account posting one-star reviews of businesses it never served; an off-topic rant with no connection to your business; spam carrying links or promotions; or a review of a completely different company. In those cases, flag the content through your Google Business Profile dashboard and let Google evaluate it. The litmus test is simple: is the review dishonest or off-topic, or is it merely unflattering? Unflattering does not qualify. A truthful negative review is exactly the content these policies exist to protect, so report only what genuinely breaks the rules.

Review velocity versus average rating: why you need both

Two numbers dominate your review dashboard, and owners tend to obsess over the wrong one. Your average rating is the star number that appears next to your business name — a snapshot of your reputation over the whole life of the listing. Your review velocity is the pace at which new reviews arrive, week by week and month by month. Both matter, and they do different jobs.

Rating: the visible gatekeeper

Your average rating is the most visible number in local search — the stars that stop a prospect mid-scroll in the map pack. Keep the average comfortably above four stars and you stay in the game wherever reputation matters. The biggest jump in trust happens in the move from a middling rating to a good one, not in the climb from excellent to flawless, which is why obsessing over a perfect five is a mistake. A believable high-four with honest reviews beats a suspicious five-point-zero that reads like a controlled sample; a perfect score raises questions an honest score never does.

Velocity: the freshness signal

Velocity tells a different story: the story of a living business rather than a well-rated one. A profile with one hundred reviews all posted three years ago and a profile with thirty reviews posted over the last six weeks look very different to ranking systems and to prospects; recency and steady arrival read as 'this business is operating right now.' Industry research suggests the local algorithms give weight to how recently and how steadily reviews arrive, which is why a one-review-a-week habit outperforms a fifty-review-per-quarter sprint. For a typical local service business, roughly one to two genuine new reviews per week on average keeps the profile visibly active; the healthy number for you depends on your territory, your volume of finished jobs, and the intensity of the competition around you.

Why you should never buy or fake either number

Buying reviews tries to fake both numbers at once, and it collapses on all of them. Bought reviews are detectable, they skew the honest signal of your profile, and if Google removes them your lifetime total falls back to the number your real customers produced — which is exactly the number you were trying to escape. Faked velocity — a burst of ten identical five-star reviews from ten accounts in a single evening — has a distinctive footprint that review-monitoring systems are built to spot. The honest alternative to both is a habit: every job, a gentle ask, forever. Slow, real, and steady beats fast, shiny, and fake every single time, because the asset compounds only while the reviews are genuine.

Review tools and software: automate the ask, never the answer

There is a fully legitimate market for review management software, and using it well is good business. These platforms collect your direct review links, send your aftercare messages, surface new reviews as they land, and ping you the moment a negative one appears so that you can respond within hours. The right tool removes the administrative weight from your review habit and keeps your follow-up consistent, which is more than half the battle.

What automation is clearly allowed

Nearly everything about the delivery of the ask is allowed, provided the ask stays neutral. Automate the timing of your requests. Personalise the message with the customer's name and your team's name. Automate a single gentle follow-up for customers who did not respond. Route new reviews into one dashboard, and set an instant alert for anything below four stars so you can reply fast. All of that is the same honest ask, delivered by software instead of by hand — and software tends to be more consistent, which is precisely the point. The guideline that matters is simple: if a customer could not tell whether a human or a workflow sent the message, and they would not care either way, you have automated well.

Where the line sits

Now the uncomfortable part. There are firms that sell services uncomfortably close to the line, priced and phrased in friendly language — 'guaranteed five-star reputation,' 'review generation packages,' 'review removal.' The phrasing sounds like software; the mechanism is usually fabricated content or purchased accounts. Pay-per-review marketplaces, reciprocal review swaps, and software that plants reviews from invented or borrowed accounts all sit outside Google's policies, and they trade your real accumulated reviews against a short window of fake brightness. If a provider cannot explain plainly how its reviews are produced, assume the honest answer would not survive the telling.

A red-flag checklist

  • Promises a specific star rating rather than simply more genuine reviews.
  • Cannot identify the real customers behind the reviews it delivers.
  • Filters or hides negative feedback before it ever reaches your dashboard.
  • Posts reviews on behalf of customers instead of asking customers to post their own.
  • Prices drop sharply as volume rises, which usually means the reviews are bulk-produced.
  • Offers to remove or suppress reviews you did not ask about.

The cost-benefit reality

Here is the honest math. A modest tool that automates your aftercare message and review invite can pay for itself by saving your team an hour a week and, more importantly, by making sure the ask never gets skipped when the office is busy. But no software manufactures trust, and the instant a tool tries to, it converts a compounding asset into a liability with a deletion date on it. Whatever platform you choose, keep the honesty in-house: software for the delivery, people for the relationship, and never outsource the truth.

Reviews across platforms: Google first, but not Google only

Google reviews should be your primary target because they feed your map visibility and your search presence in a way nothing else quite matches. But an attentive local business treats reviews as a system, not a single platform, because customers form impressions in several places at once. Facebook reviews sit beside your business page for the social audience. Industry directories carry weight in trades like roofing, HVAC, and home services. Marketplaces and third-party rating sites shape decisions before a prospect ever reaches your site. A steady, honest stream everywhere you appear tells one consistent story, and some platforms cross-pollinate — a strong reputation on Facebook can pull in customers who then search you on Google and add their review there.

The plus-ones and engagement signals

One practical point worth acting on: Google's ecosystem rewards profiles that people engage with. When customers like, connect, and repeatedly interact with your profile, those interaction signals reinforce the same 'active and trusted' story your reviews are already telling. The same logic applies everywhere else — a post about finished work that people respond to, a follow-up message that people actually answer, a website that loads fast and converts. Every surface where your business behaves like a real, operating, responsive concern strengthens the case your reviews have already made.

Photos: the quiet engagement asset

Industry research has for years linked photo activity with higher engagement, and the mechanism is easy to see: profiles with fresh, genuine photos give prospects a reason to linger, to click, and to browse inside the listing instead of leaving. A profile with a steady gallery — your team, the vans, finished jobs, the shop, honest before-and-after shots — reads as a living business, and the browsing signals that result reinforce the same reputation your reviews describe. Add a handful of genuine photos a month, keep them current, and let the gallery do the talking for the work your words have already promised.

Answer the Q&A section before customers have to ask

The questions and answers section of your Google Business Profile is an underrated slice of the same reputation surface. Prospects ask the questions they always ask — service area, pricing ballpark, insurance, call-out charges, opening hours — and you can answer them yourself, in your own voice, before a visitor has to type a word. Google has been known to surface those common questions in search results, so a well-answered Q&A becomes one more asset that travels with your business. Keep the answers brief, accurate, and consistent with your profile hours and services. Measuring your review program like a marketer

What gets measured gets improved, and review programs are no exception. The metrics that matter are few and simple, and you can track them in a spreadsheet in less time than it takes to read this section.

The three numbers to watch every week

  • New reviews per week. This is your velocity. A quiet week is fine if the ask still happened; a busy week built on incentives is a failure regardless of the count.
  • Average rating. Watch the trend, not the single value. A dip from a 4.9 to a 4.8 after your twentieth genuine review is not an emergency; a trendline sliding for two months is a signal about service quality, not about the review system.
  • Response time. How fast, on average, you reply to a new review. Speed matters most on negative reviews, but answering everything promptly signals attentiveness to the prospects who read your threads.

Tie reviews to outcomes, not just to activity

Reviews are a means, not the end. Take a monthly snapshot of your map pack position for your core search terms and sit it next to your review activity for the same period. Industry research suggests a broad, positive relationship between review momentum and local visibility over time; if your position trends upward while reviews flow steadily, that is your local search story in a single picture. Now watch the conversion side too: calls, direction requests, and website clicks from your profile (all visible in the insights section of your Google Business Profile) tend to move in the same direction as a growing, healthy review profile, which turns review work into revenue you can point at.

When to invest more

The moment you have both a dependable review habit and clear signs it is working is the moment to double down on everything else that compounds it: photos, Q&A, consistent hours, strong site content, and the wider citation work. Reviews are one leg of a local ranking stool, and you get the most from this investment when the other legs are solid too. Common mistakes small businesses make with reviews

Every mistake below is common, and every one is avoidable with a moment of reflection:

  • Gating by rating. Asking only your happy customers, or phrasing the ask so unhappy customers self-select out. It skews your reviews, it sits outside the spirit of the guidelines, and it trains you to hide problems instead of fixing them.
  • Incentivising reviews. Offering a discount, a gift, a freebie, or entry into a prize draw in exchange for a review. Google discourages reviews given in exchange for value, so the reward is a paperweight that could cost you real reviews later.
  • The five-star ask. 'If you loved the job, leave us five stars!' — the phrase most owners use without thinking, which quietly requests only positive reviews. Ask for 'a review,' not 'a five-star review.'
  • Buying reviews. Paying a service, a friend, or a customer for reviews. It is detectable, it is against policy, and the cleanup when it is unmasked is far more expensive than the honest habit.
  • Responding defensively to negative reviews. Arguing in public, calling the customer a liar, demanding an edit — each of these does more visible damage than the review itself.
  • Ignoring your own follow-up. Asking once, hearing nothing, and never sending the gentle reminder — the single most common reason entire programs decay to zero.
  • Review swapping with other businesses. 'I will review your shop if you review mine.' Mutual admiring audiences are manipulation, and the reviews themselves are usually thin and unconvincing.
  • Losing consistency. Running review pushes in bursts — a month of energy followed by six months of silence. The ranking systems reward the steady habit, not the sprint.

Read the list once more and notice the pattern: every mistake is a shortcut. There are no shortcuts worth taking in review reputation, because the value lives in the trust — and trust has no substitute.

The bottom line

Getting more Google reviews in 2026 is not a hack and not a campaign; it is a habit. Ask every customer, at the right moment, through the shortest possible path, and keep doing it for as long as you operate the business. Respect the guidelines the way you value the reviews themselves — no incentives, no gating, no fakes, no purchased five-stars. Meet your negative reviews with the same warmth you would want aimed at you, and read them closely, because they are your cheapest market research. Measure what you do each week, and let a steady rhythm of honest reviews do what it has always done: put a trustworthy business in front of the neighbours searching for one.

Frequently asked questions

How do I ask customers for a Google review without breaking the rules?+

Keep the ask simple, neutral, and universal. Ask every customer, not just the happy ones, for a genuine review at a natural moment such as right after the job or the paid invoice. Provide the direct review link from your Google Business Profile or a QR card, and follow up once or twice with a gentle reminder if they said they would write one. Do not offer rewards, discounts, or prizes in exchange, and do not ask for a five-star review specifically. You are inviting honest feedback, and that is exactly what Google’s guidelines protect.

How many Google reviews do I need to show up in the local map pack?+

There is no magic number, and Google does not publish a threshold. More genuine reviews, a strong average rating, and steady review velocity all help your profile compete alongside proximity, relevance, profile completeness, and website signals. For a typical service business, a reliable habit of one to two new genuine reviews a week builds the kind of momentum that makes a visible difference over a few months. Aim for a defensible average well above four stars and a steady, honest flow, and let the map position be the scoreboard rather than the goal.

Can I offer a discount or gift card in exchange for a Google review?+

No. Google’s guidelines discourage reviews that are given in exchange for value, and a business that offers a discount or prize for a review risks having those reviews removed. A reputation built on incentivised feedback is exactly the kind that falls apart under scrutiny. If you want to reward customers, reward everyone regardless of whether they review, and never make the review a condition of the reward. The cleanest approach is no transactional strings at all: ask every customer genuinely, and let the reviews be the honest record of your work.

How should I respond to a negative Google review?+

Promptly, warmly, and specifically. Thank the reviewer for taking the time, acknowledge the exact issue they described, briefly correct any factual error without arguing, and move the resolution to a private channel such as a phone call or a direct message. Answer within a day or two at most. Never argue in public, never ask the reviewer to edit or remove the review, and never promise something you cannot deliver. A thoughtful public reply shows every future prospect how you handle problems, and that is often worth more than the review itself.

How often should I ask customers for reviews?+

Ask after every completed job that carries a real success signal, meaning the work is done, the invoice is paid, and the customer is satisfied, and use the strongest single moment per job rather than several weak attempts. One clear ask is the primary move; one gentle reminder days later is fine for customers who said they would review but did not. Beyond that, further nudging becomes annoying and starts to look pushy. Consistency matters far more than frequency: a business that asks after every job, week after week, year after year, will out-review a business that runs intense bursts of asking and then stops.
