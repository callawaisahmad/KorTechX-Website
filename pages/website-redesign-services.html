Website Redesign Services: When You Need One (and How to Do It Without Losing SEO)

🗓️ Sep 27, 2026⏱️ 16 min read✍️ KorTechX Team
KT
Written by the KorTechX Engineering Team
Engineers and SEO specialists who build hand-coded, high-performance websites, apps and AI automation for growing businesses across the US, Canada, UK, Australia, New Zealand and the UAE.

Nobody approves a website redesign because shopping for one is enjoyable. They approve it because the site has quietly stopped doing its job. It looks dated, it drags on a phone, three competitors now look sharper than you do, and the enquiries it used to produce have thinned out. What you are buying is not a nicer looking website. It is a fix to a commercial problem, and that is the standard every redesign should be measured against.

There is one objection that stops more projects than price ever will, and it deserves a straight answer: will a redesign cost me my Google rankings? A poorly managed redesign can cost you traffic. A properly managed one usually improves it, because the same work that fixes your design also fixes the templates, speed and structure that were holding your rankings back. The difference between those two outcomes is a process, and the process is not complicated. Protect your URLs. Migrate your content properly. Keep titles and headings pointing at the same search intent. Ship a site at least as fast as the old one. Watch Search Console for weeks afterwards, not days. Any provider who treats those steps as optional extras has already told you how the project will go.

What follows covers what a real website redesign involves: the signs you have outgrown your current site, how to choose between a refresh, a redesign and a rebuild, the steps that protect your search visibility, what the work costs in 2026, and what to have ready before you brief anyone. If you already suspect you know the answer, jump to the cost section. If you are holding quotes, read the process section, because process is what separates a redesign that improves your SEO from one that quietly undoes years of work.

Key takeaways

  • A redesign is a rebuild of the parts that matter, not a repaint. If the templates, structure and content are sound, a refresh is enough. If they are not, changing colours will not fix the problem.
  • Losing rankings is a process failure, not an inevitability. URL mapping, content migration, preserved titles, equal or better speed and post-launch monitoring keep your visibility intact. Ask any provider to spell out how they handle each one.
  • Most redesigns are triggered by commercial symptoms, not vanity. Falling traffic, low conversion, a broken mobile experience and a platform nobody can maintain are all legitimate reasons to spend.
  • Scope decides price more than page count does. A five-page site and a forty-page site can cost the same or wildly different amounts, because what is being rebuilt differs. Get the scope written down before comparing quotes.
  • You should finish owning more than you had before. Clean code, a documented CMS, a faster site and a redirect map are assets. Insist on them in writing before the final invoice.

Signs you need a redesign

Redesigns are triggered by a short list of symptoms far more often than by a change of taste. If two or more of the following are true, you are no longer looking at a refresh problem.

  • The design reads as dated. Visibly out of step with the industry you sell in. Buyers form a credibility judgement in seconds, and a site that looks a decade old loses that judgement quietly.
  • The technology underneath is old or unmaintainable. A platform that stopped being updated, a page builder that no longer installs cleanly, a theme with no security patches, or code nobody can edit safely. Every future change becomes expensive and risky.
  • The site is slow. Poor loading performance is a design and engineering decision, not bad luck. It affects how you rank, how your advertising performs, and how many people wait around on a mobile connection.
  • Mobile is broken or an afterthought. Text too small, buttons hard to hit, forms that will not complete, navigation that collapses into something unusable. Most of your visitors meet you on a phone first.
  • Conversion is low despite decent traffic. People arrive and leave. The offer is unclear, the path to enquiry is too long, trust signals are missing, or the call to action asks for too much too soon.
  • The business itself has changed. New brand, new name, a merger, a new service line, a new market. The site now describes a business that no longer exists in that form.
  • You need to move platforms. Your current CMS is expensive, hard to export from, or held by an agency that treats you as a hostage. Migration is a good moment to fix design and UX at the same time.
  • Rankings or traffic are declining. A gradual slide, a drop after a core update, or fewer pages ranking for terms you used to own. Before blaming content, check whether technology and structure are the cause.

Redesign vs refresh vs rebuild: picking the right scope

These three words get used interchangeably in sales conversations, and they describe three very different projects at three very different prices. Settling this before you start is the cheapest cost control available to you.

How to redesign without losing SEO

Search engines do not penalise redesigns. They penalise broken sites: pages that vanish, content that disappears, titles that change meaning, and experiences that get slower. The fundamentals in Google's own SEO starter guide have stayed stable for years. Every step below prevents one of those failures. Ask any provider how they handle each one, because the answers will tell you more than the portfolio will.

Keep your URL structure, or map every old URL to a new one

Your URLs are often the most durable asset you own in search. A page that has ranked for years can lose most of that value in a single afternoon if its address changes and nothing points to the new location.

  • A changed URL with no redirect is a deleted page. Search engines treat it as a removal, rankings fade over weeks rather than overnight, and the traffic does not return when you change it again.
  • A 301 redirect passes the signal. A permanent redirect says the page has genuinely moved, so accumulated authority can follow it instead of evaporating.
  • Unlinked old URLs still get clicked. Your addresses appear in emails, invoices, quotation PDFs, social posts and other people's backlinks. A redirect map catches that traffic and stops it hitting error pages.

The rule we work to: if the new page serves the same purpose as the old one, keep the old URL and reorganise your navigation and internal linking instead. That delivers nearly all of the benefit with none of the risk. Where a URL genuinely must change, build a complete old-to-new map, apply 301s, and keep the map in the project documentation so anyone can audit it later.

Migrate content properly rather than copying it blindly

Content is where rankings actually live. The most common migration mistake is exporting what exists without asking whether it should still exist, which is how sites end up with three competing pages about the same service and a contact page that ranks for nothing.

  • Consolidation beats duplication. Several weak pages competing for the same phrase usually lose to one strong page. Merging them is a gain, provided the merged page is genuinely better.
  • Intentional removals beat thin filler. Deleting a page that earns nothing and pointing internal links elsewhere is cleaner than keeping it alive for appearances.
  • Formatting carries meaning. Headings, internal links, alt text, structured data and image references all travel with the content. A migration that strips them quietly removes relevance signals.

Keep titles, headings and search intent exactly where they are

The wording of your title tag and your H1 is not a design decision. It tells search engines what a page is for. If a new template changes that wording, shifts the intent, or buries the H1 three sections down the page, you are asking the ranking to start again.

  • Intent matching drives placement. If a page ranks for a specific phrase and the redesign changes the angle, you lose that placement even if the new page is objectively better.
  • It is cheap to preserve. Keeping proven titles costs nothing. Rewriting them should be a deliberate decision, tested afterwards rather than assumed.
  • Headings help readers too. Clear heading structure assists scanners and buyers at once, which is a rare case where one fix serves both.

Ship a site that is at least as fast as the old one

Loading performance is one of the easiest wins in a redesign, and one of the most common ways to undo one. A beautiful design built with oversized images, heavy sliders, five web fonts and a pile of third-party scripts loses ground you had already gained. This is an engineering problem as much as a design one, which is why we treat performance as part of our web development process rather than an optional extra. The three metrics that decide the score are set out in the Core Web Vitals guide.

  • Speed affects ranking and advertising spend at once. Slower pages cost you visibility in organic search and lower conversion on every advertising pound or dollar you spend.
  • Third-party tags are the usual culprit. Analytics, chat widgets, heat maps and marketing tools add weight you do not control. Audit them and load them properly rather than deleting the tools you need.
  • Performance decays without maintenance. New content, embeds and plugins quietly slow a site down, so agree what gets measured after launch.

Re-submit sitemaps and clean up the index

A redesign usually changes how pages are discovered. Regenerate the XML sitemap, submit it in Google Search Console and Bing Webmaster Tools, confirm robots directives are correct, and check that every page is indexable and canonical to itself.

  • The sitemap is a direct instruction. It tells search engines what exists and what has changed, instead of leaving them to work it out through broken links.
  • Stale index entries waste crawl attention. Old URLs sitting in the index as errors keep pointing at pages that no longer exist.

Measure before and after, and agree what happens next

Capture your current rankings, organic sessions, conversion rate and speed metrics before a single file changes. Then check the same numbers weekly for at least a month afterwards. Small dips are normal in any rebuild. Anything larger is a signal to act, and only a recorded baseline lets you tell a wobble from a problem.

  • Without a baseline, every result looks like a result. You cannot improve on a number you never recorded, or diagnose a fall you did not predict.
  • It defines who is responsible. Agree in writing how long monitoring is included, what triggers a fix, and how quickly that fix happens.

What the redesign process looks like

A well-run redesign is a sequence rather than a surprise. Each stage produces something you can see and approve, which keeps feedback useful and stops the project drifting into endless revisions.

StageDeliverable
AuditTechnical and SEO audit, analytics and Search Console review, content inventory, competitor comparison, and a prioritised list of what is working and what is not. This stage sets the scope and the budget.
WireframesLow-fidelity layouts for every template, approved before any visual design begins, plus a final sitemap and navigation model. This is the cheapest stage in which to change your mind.
DesignHigh-fidelity designs for the templates that matter most, a style guide covering type, colour, spacing, forms and states, and design for mobile, error and empty states rather than only the homepage.
BuildHand-coded development on your existing platform or a new one, CMS setup, forms and notifications, on-page SEO foundations, a performance budget and analytics tracking. Our web development process sets out that stage in detail.
QA on stagingA private test environment reviewed on real devices, browsers and connection speeds, with cross-browser checks, form testing, an accessibility pass, link checks and a full redirect test.
Launch with redirectsFinal backup, DNS cutover, redirect map applied and verified, analytics and Search Console confirmed, SSL in place, and a rollback plan nobody should ever need.
Post-launch monitoringDaily checks for the first fortnight, then weekly across the agreed window: crawl errors, rankings, traffic, conversion rate and speed, with fixes included rather than billed as extras.

Two questions tell you whether a provider runs this properly. First, what happens if the site performs worse after launch than before: who fixes it, how quickly, and is that in the contract. Second, who owns the project day to day, because the outcome tracks the consistency of the person managing it far more closely than the seniority of the person who pitched it.

Why website design and web development belong to the same team

Every stage from build to launch is web development, not decoration: frontend templates, backend logic, CMS setup your team can publish from, migrations that break no URLs, and a staging build reviewed before launch. That is what separates website development from a design handover, and where web development for business shows up in enquiries.

Look for a web development agency owning both halves: design and development under one accountable team. A web design agency that hands files to a separate developer means two conversations and one confused visitor. Compare website development agencies on who owns the whole launch, because dropped pieces are usually unowned ones.

Ask each supplier to list their website development services as separate line items, because full website development agency services put design, build, migration, launch and support under one contract and one point of accountability.

What to prepare before briefing your redesign team

Redesign projects run faster and cost less when the client arrives organised. Everything below is normally sitting in one of your inboxes or a folder on somebody's laptop, and having it ready removes days of back and forth.

  • Your goals and the data behind them. What has to change in the next twelve months, and what number proves it? More qualified enquiries, a better cost per lead, a launch date, a tender requirement, a funding round. Bring the analytics, not just the ambition.
  • Access to analytics and search tools. Google Analytics, Search Console, Bing Webmaster Tools, your CRM, your call tracking. A provider who cannot see your data will design around their own assumptions.
  • A content inventory. Every page, what it currently does for the business, whether it earns anything, and whether it should survive, be merged, be rewritten or be removed. The single most useful document you can bring to a redesign.
  • Competitor and inspiration references. Two or three sites you admire is enough, provided you say what you like about each: the structure, the tone, the pricing page, the industry feel. Far more efficient than a mood board of forty screenshots.
  • Brand assets. Logos in vector format, colour values, fonts with valid licences, photography, tone-of-voice guidance, and any compliance requirements around claims, regulated sectors or accessibility.
  • A list of what must not break. The integrations that pay you, the URLs customers have bookmarked, the forms your team relies on, the tracking finance reports on, and the pages carrying your strongest backlinks. Put it in writing on day one.
  • Decisions on the awkward questions. Who signs off, how many revision rounds are included, what happens if scope changes, who writes the copy, and what the launch date genuinely is. Projects stall on these, not on design.

The bottom line

A website redesign is not a cosmetic purchase. It is a rebuild of the asset that brings people to your business, convinces them you are credible, and then asks for the sale. Done properly it comes out faster, cleaner, better structured for search, easier for your team to run, and entirely yours to keep.

Done carelessly, it costs you rankings you spent years earning and hands you a site that is harder to use than the one it replaced. The variable is not luck and it is not the size of the budget. It is process: URL preservation, deliberate content migration, retained titles, performance parity, a complete redirect map, and monitoring that continues after the invoice is paid. Insist on all six, in writing, before you sign.

Frequently asked questions

Will a website redesign make me lose my Google rankings?+

Not if it is managed properly. The rankings most at risk come from four specific failures: URLs changing without 301 redirects, content being dropped or duplicated, titles and headings being rewritten in a way that shifts search intent, and the new site shipping slower than the old one. A provider who preserves or correctly maps your URLs, migrates content deliberately, keeps proven titles and headings, matches or improves speed, and monitors Search Console for several weeks after launch will usually see rankings hold or improve, because the rebuild fixes the structure and performance issues that were limiting them. Ask any redesign company to explain their redirect plan and their post-launch monitoring period in writing before you sign.

How often does a business website need redesigning?+

Most owners get two to three meaningful redesigns in a ten-year window, so roughly every three to five years for a business whose services, market and technology move at a normal pace. The calendar is a weak guide, though. Redesign when something is actually broken: a design that undermines credibility, a mobile experience that frustrates visitors, conversion that has stalled, a platform that can no longer be maintained or exported, or rankings that are sliding. Redesigning because a style has moved on is the most common way businesses spend money without a return, and a well-executed refresh is often the right answer for a year or two more.

How much does a website redesign cost in 2026?+

For custom work rather than templates, expect roughly the low five figures for a small site of five to ten pages, mid-range five figures for a medium marketing site of fifteen to forty pages with several templates and integrations, and upper five figures into six figures for large or e-commerce projects with a catalogue, checkout, accounts or a platform migration. Small sites typically take six to ten weeks, medium sites two to four months, and large projects three to six months. Scope moves price far more than page count does, so compare quotes only when deliverables, template count and content work are written down.

How long does a website redesign take?+

Budget six to ten weeks for a small business site, two to four months for a medium marketing site, and three to six months for e-commerce or a complex migration, measured from an agreed brief rather than the first conversation. The main causes of delay are late content decisions, slow feedback and approvals, an unclear scope, and content being written during the build rather than before it. A phased launch is worth considering on larger projects, because the highest-value pages can go live sooner while the rest of the site follows, protecting both your search visibility and your cash flow during the project.

Can we redesign the site and stay on our current platform?+

Yes, and it is often the sensible choice. A redesign is mainly about structure, templates, UX and design, all of which can be rebuilt on an existing content management system when that system is stable, supported and exportable. Staying put keeps your content and URLs in place, shortens the timeline and usually costs less. Move platform when the current one is expensive to license, impossible to export from, no longer maintained, fundamentally unable to do something your business needs, or so awkward to edit that your team avoids using it. Either way, confirm the export format and make sure you own the content, the code and the hosting before work starts.

Does the redesign include the actual web development work?+

Yes, and it should be written into the scope. A redesign is design plus real website development, and the engineering work is usually the larger half of it: rebuilding templates on clean code, configuring the CMS so your team can publish, migrating content and URLs, connecting forms and any CRM, booking or payment integrations, and proving all of it on a private staging site before launch. So decide whether you are buying a design that someone else will build, or end-to-end website development agency services that carry the rebuild as well. If a proposal covers design only, a second supplier has to construct the site, and the handover is exactly where scope and quality get lost. Ask for the build stages to be named individually, and for the handover to state what you own at the end: the code, the CMS access, the redirect map and the written documentation.

Usually as one scope-based fixed quote covering both design and the rebuild, with the development portion shown inside it rather than buried. A broadly typical split is a design fee for wireframes, templates and the style guide, and a larger build fee for the code, CMS, integrations, migration, staging and launch, though the ratio moves with the project: a template-led site can sit nearer two-thirds design, while a platform migration or an e-commerce build pushes most of the spend into development.
