Web Design for Service Businesses: Build a Site That Books Jobs, Not Just Browsers

🗓️ Sep 14, 2026⏱️ 31 min read✍️ KorTechX Team
KT
Written by the KorTechX Engineering Team
Engineers and SEO specialists who build hand-coded, high-performance websites, apps and AI automation for growing businesses across the US, Canada, UK, Australia, New Zealand and the UAE.

Web design for service businesses means building a website that books jobs, not one that simply looks respectable. When a person lands on your site they usually have a broken tap, a stuck door or a stranded van, so the page either moves them toward a booked job in seconds or loses them to the next name in the search results. Treat the site as a job-booking machine: every page, button and line of copy either gets a caller, a quote request or a confirmed booking, or it is in the way.

Key takeaways

  • Give every service its own page with one job to do and one call to action, instead of cramming everything onto a single all-purpose page.
  • Put click-to-call on every number and keep a sticky call bar on mobile, because emergency and same-day work is won in seconds.
  • Treat speed as a salesperson: Google's Core Web Vitals thresholds are published, and a slow mobile page sends customers straight back to the results.
  • Lead with reviews, real photos, licence numbers and guarantees, because customers book tradespeople they trust, not tradespeople they found by luck.
  • Set the local SEO foundations, matching name, address and phone everywhere and linking a verified Google Business Profile, so the pages you build can be found at all.

The service-page anatomy that converts

Most service business websites fail the same way. There is a homepage, a contact page, and one long services page where every offering gets a two-line paragraph, and the visitor is expected to work out which part applies to them. Given a choice between doing that work and tapping the next result, most visitors tap the next result.

The fix is to give each service its own page and build every one of them like a pitch, not a description. Emergency towing and detailing packages are different jobs, sold to different customers in different moments, and they need different pages. A tow truck page written for a car owner stranded at 2am will not work as a page selling a Saturday morning detail, and neither will one page pretending to do both.

Start by listing the services that actually pay the rent, not the ones you theoretically offer. A garage owner may get asked about tyres and towing both, but the structure should favour what actually books: if towing is the money line, towing sits at the top of the menu, gets the dedicated page and owns the sticky call number, in that order.

One page, one job, one CTA

Before writing anything, define the single job the page exists to do. Someone searching drain cleaning near me at nine in the morning has a slow sink and a day to salvage. Someone searching 24 hour towing at two in the morning is standing on a hard shoulder. The page that answers the exact thought and offers the exact next step wins, whether that step is a call, a quote form or a booking slot.

Here is the mapping we use when we design sites for trades and home-service clients. You can copy the pattern directly:

Notice how the call to action changes with the job. Emergency pages lead with the phone. Price-driven pages lead with the quote form. Appointment-driven pages lead with time slots. If you look at a page and cannot tell which of those three the writer chose, the page is not finished.

Name pages the way your customer talks

Navigation labels and page titles should copy the words your customer types into the search box, drain cleaning, emergency towing, rekey, not the clever name marketing would prefer. A man looking for a locksmith at midnight does not tap Access Engineering; he taps 24 Hour Locksmith. When the label on a button, the heading on a page and the phrase in the search result all match, the customer feels understood in the first second, and that first second decides most of the job.

The homepage has exactly two jobs: say what you do in one line and route everyone to the right page. Nothing else belongs above the fold. A visitor looking for drain cleaning should never have to read your company story to find the drain page; one clear link or a searchable menu does it.

Button copy is a headline wearing a small font. Call Now for a 24 Hour Reply outsells Submit the same way Book My Slot outsells Click Here, because the customer reads the button as a promise of what happens next. Write buttons as offers, not as commands.

TL;DR: one page, one job, one clear call to action, and nothing else competing for the visitor's attention.

Mobile and the one-tap call

Service business traffic is mobile traffic, and not in the polite will-read-it-later way. Your customers search from the kitchen with a leaking tap, from a van between jobs, or from the roadside waiting on a tow. The whole buying decision can happen in a phone browser in under two minutes, so the site has to work as well in the doorway of a van as it does on a desktop monitor.

Start with the phone number. Every number on the site should be a tappable link, click-to-call, a tel link, rather than plain text, so a single tap places the call. Then make the call impossible to miss on mobile with a sticky call bar pinned to the bottom of the screen, so the number stays reachable no matter how far the customer scrolls through reviews or photos.

Designed for one thumb

Most mobile visitors hold the phone in one hand, with a thumb parked on the lower half of the screen. That is where the sticky call bar belongs, and it is why tap targets should be comfortably large rather than stylishly small. If a button is hard to hit while you are tired, stressed and holding a phone in a cold carpark, it is not a conversion tool, it is a wall the customer loses on.

A clear hierarchy for the call to action

Give the phone top billing for emergency and same-day services, and give the quote form or booking slot a visible secondary place. Not everyone is ready to call, and a customer who wants a written quote should never feel trapped on a page that only knows how to ring. The rule is simple: never make the customer hunt for the way forward.

Test the real experience before you ship. Open the site on an actual phone, not a resized browser window. Call the number from the homepage, submit the quote form, tap every button a customer would tap and time each step. If a phone in a gloved hand cannot reach the call bar in one move, the lead funnel has a hole in it.

When someone taps the call button, what actually happens? If the number rings a landline nobody picks up after five, or an automated menu that buries the human, the best web design in the world fails at the exact moment it should succeed. Test the phone path with the same seriousness as the pages: do calls forward to a mobile after hours, is the 24 hour number genuinely staffed or politely routed to voicemail, and does a missed call get a callback inside your published promise.

The contact page is part of the machine

Show the working hours, the call-out policy and the service radii on one screen, with the number, the quote form and the emergency line all visible without scrolling. If you work from a van, say exactly where the van is based, because a customer who can picture where help is coming from calls with more confidence.

For drive-to businesses like car washes and detailing, add a button that hands the coordinates straight to the phone's map app, so a customer who searched car wash near [street name] can arrive without asking anything of your site.

If you run the business solo, say so honestly: one van, one owner, one phone, answered directly. Solo operators who state it plainly convert precisely because they do not pretend to be a fleet, and a customer who likes the idea of one accountable human books faster than a customer drowning in corporate promises.

Speed as a salesperson

Speed is usually the last thing a trade business designs for and the first thing its customers feel. If a towing site takes four seconds to show its phone number while the driver sits on a freeway shoulder, the driver searches for the next towing company. There is no review page, no price match and no loyalty card in that moment, only a number that did not appear fast enough.

The Core Web Vitals that matter most

Google measures experience with Core Web Vitals, and the two that matter most for service sites are LCP and INP. LCP, largest contentful paint, is how long visitors wait before the main content becomes visible. INP, interaction to next paint, is how quickly the page responds when a visitor taps or scrolls. Google's published thresholds judge a good LCP at under 2.5 seconds and a good INP at under 200 milliseconds, public numbers rather than marketing guesswork.

If your pages sit in the poor column of Search Console's Core Web Vitals report, real mobile visitors are paying for it in waiting time, and so are you in unplaced calls. Search Console and PageSpeed Insights are both free, and they measure what your actual users experience rather than what the office laptop feels like.

Why three seconds loses jobs

Industry research commonly cites three seconds as the point where abandonment climbs sharply, and the exact figures vary from study to study, but the direction never does: the longer a page takes, the more people leave. For a service business, every abandoned visit was a phone call that never happened. A job that starts with bad luck, a slow site and a dead line is already lost to the competitor who answered first.

What usually makes a trades site slow

  • Oversized photos uploaded straight from a phone or camera, never compressed.
  • A heavy page builder that ships its whole toolkit for what is basically a brochure.
  • Autoplay hero video downloading megabytes before the call number appears.
  • A stack of tracking scripts, chat widgets, maps and font libraries fighting over one connection.

Images are the usual culprit

On most trades sites we audit, the hero image alone is bigger than the whole page of text, because a photo from a modern phone is five megabytes and nobody compressed it. Resize every image to the size it actually displays, rarely more than 1,600 pixels wide and far less for a thumbnail, and serve modern formats the browser supports. That is cheap labour, not engineering, and it is where most speed problems quietly die.

Measure before you argue, then squeeze

Run every money page through PageSpeed Insights and check the field data in Search Console before blaming the hosting. The cheapest fix in most small service sites is simply shrinking the images, and that one change regularly moves a page from poor to good.

Run the numbers once a month, not once a year. Field data drifts as content grows, new scripts arrive with a plugin update, and a service page added last month can quietly triple its load time. Ten minutes in Search Console each month keeps the speed work honest.

And do not forget the human bottleneck: a fast page that lands on an answering machine at nine during an emergency is a fast page that fails anyway. Speed should buy you the customer's attention, and the phone should keep it.

Trust signals that turn browsers into bookings

Customers are handing a stranger the keys, the house or the car, so they scan for a reason to believe you before they ever call. A fast, faceless website loses the job to a slower site with a face, a licence number and a wall of real reviews. Trust is not a section of the site, it is the whole argument.

Ask yourself with every design choice whether it makes it easier for a nervous stranger to say yes. If the answer is no, it is decoration, and decoration gets cut.

Real reviews, shown early

Show your Google rating and link straight to the reviews, placed near the call to action, not buried on a separate page. A real average rating with a real count of reviews convinces the way a claim like rated best in the area, with nothing behind it, never can. If the rating is lower than you would like, put the honest number up anyway and fix the work, because hiding reviews reads as guilt.

Real photos of real jobs

Your own photos beat stock photography every time. A shot of your actual van with the company name, your technician mid-job in a real kitchen, and the finished corner of the room tells a stranger what they need: this company exists, works here, and shows up looking the part. A site full of downloaded smiling tradespeople leaves a question hanging, who am I actually getting, and nobody books a stranger.

Licences, insurance and guarantees in plain view

State the licence and insurance numbers plainly, and state the guarantee plainly too: what it covers, for how long, and what the customer gets if the work is not right, such as a free revisit or a redo within thirty days. Those specific details are what let a nervous customer say yes instead of asking three more hesitant questions.

Before and after, honestly

Before-and-after photos are the most convincing asset a cleaning or handyman business owns, because they prove a result without inventing a testimonial. Show them large with a caption explaining what the job involved. We never invent client testimonials on sites we build: real platform reviews, real photos and real guarantees are stronger, and they are also true, which has a way of mattering when fake everything is the alternative.

Say how fast you respond

If you genuinely answer within the hour, say so, and if same-day is your promise, state the hours you actually cover. Every concrete, verifiable detail you give a customer fights the fear that hiring a stranger is a gamble. Small specifics, answered in two rings, licence number shown, thirty-day redo guaranteed, do more selling than a page of adjectives.

Reviews also belong in the footer and on every money page, not only on a reviews page. The customer deciding between two similar plumbers at six in the evening notices the rating at the moment of decision, which is long after clicking a separate reviews page would have felt like an extra step.

Trade bodies and licensing authorities give you a free credibility marker. Put the membership logo and number in the footer with a link so the customer can verify you on their own time; a business that welcomes being checked out gets checked out, and then it gets called.

Local SEO foundations

A beautiful service page that nobody can find is a poster in an empty room. Local search is how service businesses are found, and the foundations are unglamorous and non-negotiable: a consistent name, address and phone number, honest service areas, and a claimed, verified Google Business Profile.

One name, one address, one phone

Google cross-references the same details across the web to decide which business is real. If the website says Johns Plumbing, Google Business Profile says Johns Plumbing Co Ltd, and a directory lists a different suburb, you have handed Google a reason to be unsure. Audit every mention and make the name, address and phone identical everywhere, starting with the footer on every page of the site.

Keep one canonical phone format, spaces, hyphens, country code, whatever you choose, and never mix a second variant into the header or footer. Little differences are still differences to a machine, and the machine is the first reader.

Set service areas honestly

Every service business has a radius where the jobs make sense, a point where travel time eats the profit. Set that radius honestly in Google Business Profile and state it plainly on the site, along the lines of we serve the greater metro from the city out to the northern suburbs. Honest service areas attract the right jobs and quietly filter out the ones you could never serve well.

Claim your Google Business Profile

If the business is not claimed and verified on Google Business Profile, that is the single highest-leverage step in local SEO, worth doing before anything else in this section. Add real photos, accurate categories, opening hours, a website link and genuine responses to reviews. Our guide walks through every field in the complete Google Business Profile guide for service businesses.

Watch the profile's own analytics

Once verified, read where Google Business Profile's own insights say customers find you, how often they use the call button, and which actions they take from the map. That is free, first-party data about what people actually do in your area, and it points straight at the pages that deserve your next hour of attention.

Titles built for the search, not the meeting

Write every page title as the answer to a question someone actually typed. Drain Cleaning in [City] with Same-Day Calls beats Professional Solutions every time, because it says what, where and how fast in a handful of words. The meta description plays the same game one level down: one sentence telling the searcher what they get and why you, and it shows in the results more often than owners realise.

Markup, titles and the feedback loop

Add LocalBusiness structured data, schema markup, to the site so search engines can read your details directly. Then close the loop with Search Console: see which pages already rank, which queries bring clicks, and which pages need work. Turning those findings into booked jobs is the subject of our guide to how local service businesses get more leads from their websites in 2026.

The areas-you-serve page pattern

The areas-we-serve pattern is one of the cheapest, most reliable wins in service business web design, and it is also the most abused. People search plumber in [suburb], tow truck [town], dog grooming near [street name]. A page that answers exactly that query, with content genuinely about that place, is a page built to be found.

The pattern, done honestly

An area page that is the same forty lines with the suburb name swapped is not local content; it is the spam that Google increasingly ignores and that real customers can smell. Write each area page as if a local had asked you a direct question: how fast can you get here, do you charge a call-out fee for this suburb, where does your team actually drive from, and what do geolocated mobile jobs look like when the van is the office.

A quick test: if ten different areas would share one paragraph, you have one paragraph pretending to be ten pages. Cut it to the three or five areas you can write properly, because what search engines punish is duplication and what customers punish is a lie dressed as local knowledge.

What belongs on an area page

Cover details only a local operator would know: typical travel and response time to that suburb, the call-out fee policy for the area, the school runs and office districts the team covers, and the landmarks that let a customer describe their location fast. Add one or two genuine photos of jobs done there. Link area pages from the footer on every page, not from a buried submenu.

Seed a few areas first, then grow

Start with the three to five suburbs where you already work most and write those pages properly. As the van covers more ground and the profile shows which searches Google already matches you for, add pages one at a time with real local detail. A handful of genuinely useful area pages ranks and converts better than forty empty ones.

Link each area page with the customer's own words: Plumbers in the North, 24 Hour Towing on the [Highway] corridor. Anchor text is free local SEO and it keeps the machine connected, because the person landing on an area page has already told you exactly what they need and where.

Home-based and van-based businesses

If you run the business from home, do not publish the home address on the site or the profile. Google Business Profile lets a service-area business hide its street address and show only its coverage area, and that is the exact setting a home-based handyman or cleaner should use. The area pages then carry the local proof that a street address would otherwise carry, so for a geolocated, van-based operation they matter even more.

What to write on each service page

Once routing, mobile and trust are sorted, the words decide the job. Here is a working template for a service page with example copy you can adapt. It is written for a drain cleaning business, but the blocks fit every trade from locksmithing to car washing.

Block one: the job, done well

Open by describing the result in the customer's terms, not your checklist of steps. Example copy: A blocked kitchen drain cleared the same day, with the pipe checked rather than merely plunged and no mess left behind. You get the cause in plain words and a fixed price before we start, so the only surprise is that there are none.

Block two: what it costs

Quoted and ranged prices calm the biggest fear in service work, the fear of being charged in the dark. Example copy: Most single-drain clearances in the metro run between a hundred and fifty and three hundred and fifty dollars including call-out, with the exact price confirmed before we touch the pipe. Emergency evening rates apply after six, quoted up front, never invented on site.

Even if you cannot quote a fixed number online, state the starting price, what it includes and the exact moment the quote becomes real. A prices-from figure that stays honest beats a glowing ask-for-a-call that quietly carries a service fee for turning up.

Block three: what a visit includes

List what the job really covers so the customer knows what they have bought. Example copy: Every booked clearance includes the inspection, a professional clearance, a camera check where it makes sense, clean-up and a written cause and prevention note. We do not upsell on the doorstep. If the pipe needs more work, you hear about it in writing, with a price, before we do it.

Block four: when to call fast

Emergency pages live or die on this block, so spell out exactly which situations warrant the urgent number. Example copy: Call the 24-hour line if water backs up onto the floor, if the drain has overflowed more than once this week, if a foul smell is working through the sinks, or if you manage tenants and cannot afford a second day of inconvenience. Anything else can wait for business hours.

Block five: the call to action, once more

End the page the way it opened, with the job that page exists to book and the button that books it. Close with honest reassurance: same-day slots most weekdays, a twenty-minute confirmation call, a real person answering. Promise only what you can genuinely keep, because a broken promise inside the page undoes every trust signal above it.

The same blocks, a different trade

Here is the same anatomy an hour later, for a locksmith. Block one: rekeyed locks in a day, new keys cut to a code you choose, old keys dead. Block two: rekeying a two-lock flat runs between one twenty and one eighty including the call. Block three: the rekey, a lock test, keys for everyone who should have them, and a written record of the codes. Block four: call the 24 hour line if a key has broken in the lock, or you are locked out with kids in the car. Block five: one action, book a window. Same skeleton, different customer, different words, because the customer's search differs.

That is the whole trick: the skeleton stays and the words change with the trade and the neighbourhood. A cleaning business leads with honesty about what the visit includes, a towing company leads with the minutes before the truck arrives, a handyman leads with the guarantee. The template is not a cage, it is a spine.

How long should the page be

Between four and six hundred readable words is plenty for most service pages, enough to answer the standard questions and short enough that the call to action stays in view. Write the way a tradesperson explains things across a kitchen counter, not the way a brochure swallows a dictionary. And use the exact service-plus-location phrase a customer would type, in the title and in the first sentence.

Quantify what your records actually support. A towing firm can truthfully print its average arrival time; a cleaner can print the square metres covered. Numbers pulled from your own logs are not invented statistics, they are proof of how you work, and they belong in the pricing block exactly where the doubt lives.

One test decides whether the copy is finished: read it out loud. If it sounds like a person explaining something helpful across a kitchen counter, it is done. If it sounds like a press release, cut the adjectives and say the next concrete thing.

What to cut from your service site

Great service web design is mostly subtraction. Here is what we routinely delete from trades sites before they launch.

Stock photography

The generic tradesperson with a wrench held at arm's length is the stamp of a business with nothing real to show. Cut the stock. If you do not have real photos yet, use clean, honest text until you do, because one grainy photo of your real van sells more than ten glossy downloads.

Even one honest photo, the van, the team, a real job in progress, grounds everything else on the site. Real beats perfect, and customers give you far more credit for the former than they ever do for the latter.

Autoplaying anything

Autoplay video burns bandwidth exactly when the customer wants the phone number, and autoplay audio is a crime against carpark-dwellers with the volume up. Video can sell service work convincingly, but it must be a choice the visitor makes, compressed, and placed below the call to action, never in front of it.

Walls of unbroken text

Unbroken walls of text bury the phone number and the point. Chunk everything into short paragraphs, lists and subheadings, because a visitor who has to read a company biography to find the call button has already left.

Too many calls to action

Call, quote, book and newsletter screaming at once teaches the visitor that nothing is urgent. Pick the single job the page exists for and give everything else a clearly secondary place. An emergency page with one giant call button converts; the same page with five competing buttons converts nobody.

Friction dressed as professionalism

Cut forms that ask for the story of your life. A trades lead is a phone number and a postcode: ask for the service, the suburb and a phone, and nothing else until the call. Every extra field is a small wall, and on mobile each one is a fresh chance for the thumb to give up.

Sliders, popups and fake urgency

Autoplay sliders, popups that appear ahead of the call bar, countdown timers pretending the offer expires tomorrow. Each one is a delay between the customer and the call, and your customer is already on a weak connection in the rain. When in doubt, cut it, and you will rarely be wrong.

Clip the infinite homepage. A service business does not need a landing page a football field long; it needs a homepage that reaches a service page in one step and a service page that reaches a call in one tap. Anything that cannot honestly point at a booked job earns its removal.

DIY builders versus professional web design for service businesses

Can a service business build its own website on a page builder and book jobs? Yes, and plenty do. Should every service business hand the site to professionals? No, and we are a web agency saying that, which should tell you something. The honest answer: it depends on your ticket size, your workload and whether the website is your main lead engine or a supporting player.

What page builders get right

Wix, Squarespace and WordPress with a page builder are cheap, fast and genuinely adequate for a business that is early, just needs a presence, or is not sure the web can even deliver calls. A good template carrying your real photos, real copy, click-to-call and a sticky call bar can absolutely book jobs. The bar for good enough is lower than most tradespeople fear, and a live decent site beats a perfect dead one.

Where the build-it-yourself ceiling shows up

Builders make speed and conversion discipline harder by default. Templates ship markup you do not need, so pages run heavier, and heavy pages bleed calls. Structured data is fiddly, the template fights your layout, and the thinking the template cannot sell you, the conversion thinking, has to come from somewhere. In our experience most trades sites that fail do not fail on design at all; they fail on strategy, meaning no page per service, no click-to-call, no honest pricing signals and no area pages. A builder hands you the tools, not the decisions.

What a professional build actually buys you

Professional web design for service businesses buys you the logic as much as the look: pages that match each service's search, speed engineered in rather than hoped for, structured data set up correctly, honest pricing and trust copy, and a launch checklist like the one below. For a business where the site sits near the top of the lead funnel, that is a business decision: the site generates a measurable slice of revenue, so it deserves a proportionate slice of budget.

If you are weighing the routes, we have written separately about web development for service businesses and how to brief, budget and build it. Whichever path you take, keep the strategy intact: one page per service, one tap to call, and a phone that gets answered.

There is a third route that we see working well: start on a builder tonight, gather real photos, reviews and call data over a few months, and move to a professional build once the site is clearly driving jobs and you know which pages actually book. The professional build then lands on truth you collected instead of guesswork, and it is easier to justify the budget when the phone already rings.

Whatever you build it in, measure from day one. Every route must share that discipline: which pages get the calls, which forms convert, which area pages appear in search. A few lines of analytics cost less than one lost job, and they tell you next month exactly where to spend your time.

Plan for the month after launch too. The busiest trades sites we see get more changes in the weeks after going live than before, because real calls reveal tiny frictions no one spotted on a desktop. Budget time, or a retainer if you hire out, for pruning dead pages and refreshing area content, because a job-booking machine only stays a machine when its gears are looked after.

The bottom line

Web design for service businesses is not a beauty contest; it is a job-booking machine built from unglamorous parts. Per-service pages that match the exact search, click-to-call and sticky mobile bars for the emergency minute, speed that respects the customer's situation, reviews and licences that build trust in seconds, local SEO foundations and area pages that let the machine actually get found, and pages that cut the noise and end on one clear call to action.

Start with your top five services, get the pages live, measure what books and iterate. A decent site that loads fast and answers the phone will beat a beautiful site that does neither, every single day of the week.

If this article distilled to three sentences, they would be: one page per service, because the search can match it; one tap to call, because the emergency is real; and one honest, concrete reason to believe you, because nothing books a job faster than a customer who stopped worrying. Everything else is craft, and craft is what we do.

Whichever route you pick, the same fundamentals win, and the parts above are those fundamentals. Frequently asked questions

How much does web design for a service business cost?+

A DIY page-builder site can go live for the cost of a monthly subscription. Paying a designer for a template site typically runs from a few hundred to a couple of thousand dollars. A professionally built, hand-coded service site usually costs more, often in the low thousands, because you are buying conversion-focused design and local SEO. Judge it against the value of one booked job: if the site books one extra job a week, most price points pay for themselves quickly.

How long does it take to get a service business website online?+

From a template, a small service business can be live in a week or two. A custom build with real content and local SEO work more commonly takes three to six weeks, because the pages need genuine copy, real photos and testing on physical phones. Delays are almost never about design; they are about content not being gathered. Collect your photos, licence numbers, pricing and reviews before the build starts, and you will be surprised how fast the site appears.

Does every service really need its own page?+

Only the services that make you money need dedicated pages, and yes, they deserve them. A garage door repair page, a drain cleaning page or an emergency towing page lets you match the exact search, write a tight call to action and compete for that phrase properly. One crowded services page forces the visitor to work out which paragraph applies to them, and most will not bother. Start with your five busiest services and add pages as capacity grows.

What is the most important feature on a trades website?+

For most service businesses it is the ability to call in a single tap. Emergency and same-day work goes to whoever picks up first, so a tappable number and a sticky mobile call button outweigh any design flourish. Fast load times and visible trust signals come next, meaning reviews, real photos, licence numbers and a clear guarantee. Prioritise in that order, and the fancy extras will have to earn their place rather than demand it.

How do I get my website to rank for my local service areas?+

Start by claiming and verifying Google Business Profile, setting accurate categories and service areas. Keep your name, address and phone number identical across the profile, the site and every directory. Then build one genuinely local page per suburb, covering response times, call-out fees and real photos of nearby jobs, rather than fifty lines of duplicated text. Watch Search Console to see which pages already attract clicks, strengthen those and add the missing area pages.
