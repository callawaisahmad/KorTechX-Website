Product Upload Services: What We Do When You Hand Us Your Catalogue

🗓️ Sep 28, 2026⏱️ 15 min read✍️ KorTechX Team
KT
Written by the KorTechX Engineering Team
Engineers and SEO specialists who build hand-coded, high-performance websites, apps and AI automation for growing businesses across the US, Canada, UK, Australia, New Zealand and the UAE.

Product upload services turn the raw material of your catalogue into a store that can actually take orders. You hand over spreadsheets, supplier exports, image folders, price lists and half-finished notes. You get back a live, structured, searchable, priced and stocked catalogue with clean titles, honest descriptions, correct variants, properly sized images, working alt text and per-product SEO. That is the whole job, and it is the reason the service exists.

Catalogue work is invisible when it is done well and expensive when it is done badly, yet it consumes more owner hours than almost anything else in a launch. A store with 600 products does not contain 600 products of work. It contains 600 small decisions about titles, categories, attributes, prices, stock and images, and each one is a chance to slow the launch down or hand a customer the wrong information. Most owners are perfectly capable of typing this data in. What they do not have is the hours, the process, or the separation of hands.

This guide explains what a professional product upload actually covers, why owners outsource it, where bulk import works and where it does not, what changes between WooCommerce and Shopify, and what you need to send us to start. It is written for a specific moment: you have either a supplier file you cannot get live, a migration in front of you, a store that is quietly under-stocked, or a launch date that keeps moving because nobody has time for the catalogue.

Key takeaways

  • You are buying a finished catalogue, not a block of data entry hours. The deliverable is a store-ready catalogue with correct structure, clean copy, right prices, live stock and per-product SEO, all of it checked before you see it.
  • Outsourcing usually wins on time and error rate before it wins on cost. The hourly maths only looks cheap in-house if you value the owner's hour at zero, which is exactly why owners end up resenting the work instead of finishing it.
  • Bulk import and manual entry are not rivals. Bulk wins on clean, consistent, already-structured data. Manual or catalogue-based entry wins on messy sources, complex variants and anything that needs judgement.
  • Platform changes the mechanics, not the standard. WooCommerce and Shopify have different import tools and field structures, but the same quality bar applies to titles, images, variants, pricing and stock on both.
  • Most upload problems are handover problems. A complete export, real images, a price list, consistent SKUs and a decided category structure remove most of the risk before a single product is typed.

What product upload services actually cover

This is the baseline list, and it is deliberately specific. The phrase we will upload your products means almost nothing until you know what sits underneath it, and anything missing from this list is an extra. If a provider cannot answer it item by item, they are guessing at your catalogue before they have seen it.

  • Data collection and normalisation. Pulling every source into one place, then standardising it, so an export full of mixed suppliers, mixed date formats and mixed category labels becomes one consistent dataset that an importer can actually read.
  • Title formatting. A consistent pattern per category, so a product reads correctly in search results, on a category page, in a cart and on a packing slip.
  • Descriptions. Writing or rewriting product copy that states what the item is, what it does, what it is made from, the specifications and the shipping and care facts, without padding it with claims you cannot support.
  • Category and attribute assignment. Mapping every product into the right category, subcategory and filter attributes, because navigation, filtering and internal search only work if this layer is right.
  • Image handling. Cleaning, cropping, resizing to platform specifications, ordering, renaming, compressing and writing alt text for every image, including the technical requirements each marketplace enforces.
  • Pricing and tax. Applying the correct price per market, including multi-currency and tax behaviour for stores selling into the US, Canada, the UK, Australia, New Zealand and the UAE.
  • Stock, SKUs and bar codes. Correct stock levels, unique and consistent SKUs, and bar codes formatted to the standard your warehouse and sales channels require.
  • Variants. Size, colour, capacity, length and bundle options mapped to the variation system the platform uses, so the options on the page behave the way customers expect.
  • Per-product SEO. Unique titles, meta descriptions, URL handles, keyword-aware copy and image alt text, so products are found in search rather than only browsed.
  • A quality assurance pass. A full review of the live catalogue for broken images, missing fields, duplicate SKUs, wrong categories, broken links and pricing errors, completed before handover rather than after your first customer complains.

Not every project needs all ten items at the same depth. A fifty-product store rarely needs a formal naming framework. A five-thousand product migration needs every one of them, and possibly twice, because the same discipline has to be applied again for each channel you sell in. Make sure the depth you are buying is written down, because this list is where scope arguments are won and lost.

Product upload is one track of the wider web development work behind a store. Theme, checkout, navigation and CMS are built in web development, and website development decides the data structures the catalogue lands in.

None of that is specialist knowledge. Unique titles, honest meta descriptions, clean handles and real alt text do most of the work, and Google’s own SEO starter guide sets out the priorities plainly.

Why businesses outsource product uploads

Owners outsource this work for four reasons, and only one of them is money. The other three are time, consistency and the specific risk of doing careful, repetitive work late at night for three consecutive weekends. The comparison below is deliberately blunt.

FactorIn-houseOutsourced
Hours per 100 productsRoughly 15 to 30 hours for a competent, careful upload, depending on image volume, variants and how clean the source data isA few hours of your time for handover and review, with the rest done in parallel by people working on other catalogues at the same time
Error rate riskRises sharply as attention drops in late sessions, and errors surface weeks later, at checkout or after a customer emailSystematic checks and a defined QA pass, because the same mistakes are found and corrected on every project rather than on yours alone
ConsistencyTitles and attributes drift over weeks, so product 40 and product 400 rarely follow the same rulesA written pattern per category applied to every product, which is what makes filtering, reporting and later migrations possible
True costYour own hours, priced at whatever you decide they are worth, and often deferred indefinitelyA fixed, agreed cost, predictable in advance, with the timeline set in writing
Speed to launchSerial, because one person is doing it in the gaps around everything elseParallel, with batches, so a launch date stops depending on evenings that never materialise

The time maths is worth doing carefully, so treat it as arithmetic rather than a promise. An owner working with clean data and a familiar platform can move quickly, sometimes 60 to 100 product fields in an hour. Now add image handling, variant mapping, category decisions, price checks and a QA pass, and a realistic figure for 100 properly listed products moves into the 15 to 30 hour range, considerably more for complex catalogues. At an hourly rate of USD 15 to 30 that is a real number. Valued at the effective rate of a founder's evening, it is a much larger one. The harder point is that this work happens before any revenue exists, which is the least favourable moment to spend your own hours.

Price is not always the deciding factor. A web development agency that built your store can also run the catalogue, so data, storefront and CMS stay owned by the same people. Comparing website development agencies often misses that, and web development for business is judged on the customer journey. A web design agency that never touches the data will re-draw the same page twice.

Manual entry vs bulk upload: what's actually practical

Both routes are legitimate and both belong in a professional service. What is not legitimate is importing a supplier file without reading it first, because a successful import is not the same thing as a clean catalogue.

Bulk upload: where it is the right tool

CSV files and platform-native importers are the fastest route to a live catalogue when the data is already clean. If every product has a title, a price, a stock level, a weight, at least one image and a sensible category, a mapped import will do in an afternoon what manual entry would take a fortnight to do. Bulk import is also repeatable, which matters for anything ongoing: new season ranges, price updates, stock corrections and re-uploads all run the same way.

Manual or catalogue-based entry: where it wins

Some catalogues cannot be imported at all. A supplier PDF, a distributor portal with no export function, photographed print catalogues, hand-written notes, a product with six options plus a size chart, bundles made from other products, or a WooCommerce variable product whose attributes differ per variant. In those cases somebody reads the source, makes the structural judgement, and creates the product properly. It is slower, and it is the part you are genuinely paying for when the source data is messy.

When hybrid wins

Most real projects are hybrid, and it is worth asking for the split in numbers rather than adjectives. A launch usually starts with one large batch that gets cleaned and imported, while a smaller group of hero or highest-margin products gets built by hand with properly written copy. Ongoing work runs as imports, with manual effort reserved for anything that breaks the pattern. A provider describing the job as entirely one or the other is describing a process they have not designed.

What a professional does differently

The difference is almost entirely in the preparation. A professional reads the file first, works out the column mapping, identifies the rows that will fail, removes merged cells and stray punctuation, fixes date and currency formats, and decides what happens to products with no image or no weight. Then they import in small test batches, inspect the result on the front end, and only then run the full job. Skipping those steps is precisely why a spreadsheet that should have saved two days ends up costing two weeks.

WooCommerce vs Shopify upload differences

The quality bar is identical on both platforms. The mechanics are not, and if your provider cannot answer this question, they are about to learn on your catalogue and on your deadline.

  • WooCommerce product types. Simple, variable, grouped and external products all behave differently, and an external product is frequently a pricing mistake as well as a catalogue one.
  • WooCommerce variations. Size, colour and other options are defined as attributes on the parent product, and each combination becomes a variation with its own SKU, price, stock and image set.
  • WooCommerce import options. The built-in importer handles standard CSV fields, while larger or repeated jobs usually justify a dedicated mapping layer, and plugin conflicts are a genuine risk worth asking about directly.
  • WooCommerce category management. Categories are hierarchical and attributes are generally global, so a large catalogue needs that structure planned before import rather than patched afterwards.
  • Shopify CSV templates. The product CSV has a fixed header with defined field names for handle, title, body, vendor, product type, tags, variants and image source, and anything outside that structure has to be mapped into it.
  • Shopify variants. Each variant is a row with its own SKU, barcode, price, compare-at price, inventory, weight and image, so variant-heavy catalogues produce long files that need testing before a full run.
  • Shopify navigation fields. Collections, tags and product types carry more of the filtering and discovery work than they do in WooCommerce, so those fields deserve real attention rather than a token tag.
  • Shopify media. Images are uploaded as files referenced by filename in the CSV, so filenames, order and file size must be correct before the import or products go live with broken galleries.
  • Shopify locations and inventory. Stock is tracked against locations, so a store serving the US, Canada, the UK, Australia, New Zealand or the UAE needs that structure configured before products are loaded.
  • When migration tools help. Moving between platforms, or out of a marketplace, a migration tool handles field translation, redirects and image handling, but it does not repair messy source data. Clean first, migrate second. Our 2026 platform comparison is worth reading before you commit to either side of that decision.

The quality rules that stop returns and refunds

A product page is a set of promises. Most returns and refund requests in a young store trace back to a promise the page made and did not keep, and the rules below prevent that at source rather than after the fact.

  • Titles that say what the product is, in the words customers actually search for, rather than a supplier code or a list of keywords nobody types.
  • Honest descriptions, covering materials, dimensions, compatibility, care and limits, and never claiming a specification the item does not have.
  • Correct attributes, because a wrong filter result walks a customer into a product that cannot do what they need, and that is a refund with a bad review attached.
  • Right pricing, including tax and shipping behaviour, so the total at checkout matches the expectation the product page set.
  • Live and accurate stock, since overselling one popular line costs you in cancelled orders, one-star reviews and customers who never come back.
  • Image resolution and consistency, with a main image showing the actual product rather than a placeholder, and enough supporting images that nobody is guessing.
  • Consistent naming per category, so filters, URLs, internal reporting and your own future staff all work from one rule instead of six competing ones.

Catalogue decisions also reach the design layer. Website designing is where a shopper should find size guidance, a stock hint and a delivery estimate, so good website design puts them next to the price rather than two screens down.

Product pages are usually the slowest pages on a store, because each one carries a gallery, a variant picker and a scripts bundle. The thresholds in the web.dev guide to Core Web Vitals apply to a category listing as much as to a home page.

Turnaround and batch sizes that work

Turnaround depends far more on data readiness and the review loop than on catalogue size. The ranges below are typical for standard product upload work with complete inputs and feedback returned within two working days.

Around 200 productsTypically 1 to 2 weeksFrequently quoted per product, often in the region of USD 4 to 12 per product for standard upload work
1,000 products or moreTypically 3 to 6 weeks, delivered in batchesUsually quoted per product in volume bands, commonly in the region of USD 3 to 9 per product depending on variant and image volume

Two things move those numbers more than size does. Missing or unusable images, and missing weights, will each add days. So will a review process where feedback arrives a week late, because every comment that lands after a batch is live becomes a correction instead of an instruction. If your launch is date-critical, agree the batch order in advance: the 200 products that earn the most should go live first, not the first 200 alphabetically. This is also the moment to consider whether the store itself needs work alongside the catalogue, which is where our e-commerce product listing service usually comes up.

Read those cost bands as upload work alone. If the store needs work alongside, that is web development cost, quoted separately rather than folded in. As a rough guide, comparable custom store builds typically run into the thousands of USD, so ask for the two lines itemised.

What you must hand over

The largest single cause of delay is a handover that is only half a handover. To get a straight quote and a realistic date, send these.

  • The product list. A full export from wherever your data lives now, including everything you would like to sell rather than only what is easy to export.
  • Images. Original files at the highest resolution you have, in a single folder, plus packaging, lifestyle and detail shots that belong on the product page.
  • Prices. Cost, retail and any regional pricing you already apply, plus how you handle tax in each market you sell into.
  • SKUs and bar codes. Your own codes and any supplier or manufacturer codes that need to be carried through into your store.
  • Weights and dimensions. Required for shipping rates, and for the delivery estimates customers use to decide whether to buy.
  • Categories. Your intended category structure, or permission to design one, since getting this right early avoids a costly restructure later.
  • Variants. The full option set for anything with size, colour, capacity or length, and the combinations you genuinely intend to stock.
  • Special rules. Free shipping thresholds, bundles, made-to-order lead times, restricted or hazmat items, products shipped from different locations, and anything that must not be discounted.

If some of this does not exist yet, say so rather than leaving it blank. A good provider will build it with you as a separately scoped part of the project, instead of absorbing it and then rushing your launch. What matters is that nothing important gets discovered halfway through. How to scope a product upload project

Five questions, asked in this order, will tell you who is quoting sensibly and what the work is really worth. Take them to every provider on your list and note where the answers turn vague.

  • Define the volume precisely. Product count alone is misleading, because variant count, image count and category count usually drive the hours. Ask for all three in writing.
  • Set the quality bar in plain terms. Who writes the descriptions, from what source, to what length, and does anyone check them? A lower quote often means thinner copy or a thinner QA pass, and you should know which one you are buying.
  • Agree the field list per product type. Write down every field that must be populated and mark the optional ones. Providers price from field lists, and a shared list settles scope disputes before they start.
  • Fix the review process. Who reviews, in what format, within how many days, and how many revision rounds are included? A single approver working from one shared document is faster than email chains and far faster than discovering problems after go-live.
  • Set the timeline around dependencies. Confirm when the data is due, when the first sample batch lands, when the live catalogue is signed off, and what happens to products that are still not ready on launch day.

One sequencing question closes this section. If the store is being built at the same time, decide early which track moves first, because a catalogue uploaded into a half-finished store usually gets rebuilt. Our web development service runs the platform build and data foundations in parallel with catalogue preparation, which is the cheapest way to avoid doing the same upload twice.

Frequently asked questions

How much do product upload services cost per product?+

There is no single market rate, because the field count and image volume drive most of the cost. As a rough guide, standard product upload work often lands somewhere between USD 3 and 12 per product, with lower per-product rates on large batches of simple products and higher rates on small batches, complex variants or products needing original descriptions. Many providers also quote a fixed minimum or setup fee, which is normal on catalogues under 50 products. Always ask whether pricing includes image preparation, description writing, SEO fields and a QA pass, because a cheaper headline rate often excludes exactly those items. We quote per product with the scope attached, so you can compare like with like.

How long does it take to upload 100 products?+

For a clean, complete data set with usable images, 100 products commonly take between three and seven working days including a quality assurance pass, and complex or variant-heavy catalogues take longer. Turnaround is driven less by the number itself than by how ready the source data is, how many variants and images each product has, and how quickly feedback comes back. Projects in the region of 200 products often run one to two weeks, and larger migrations are usually delivered in batches over several weeks. Agree a sample batch first, because it settles the real per-product pace far more accurately than any estimate.

Which platforms do you upload products to?+

We work mainly with WooCommerce and Shopify, which between them cover the majority of the stores we see, and we also handle migrations between them and imports from marketplaces. WooCommerce work involves product types, global attributes, variation structure and the choice between the built-in importer and a dedicated mapping layer. Shopify work involves the product CSV template, row-based variants, collection and tag structure, image file references and location-based inventory. The process differs by platform, but the quality bar on titles, descriptions, images, pricing, stock and SEO fields is identical, and we will tell you honestly if your platform is a poor fit for the catalogue you have.

Do we need to prepare and clean our data before sending it?+

You do not need to clean it, but you do need to send everything you have, including the messy parts. Normalising data is part of the service, and providers who import a supplier file without reading it first are the ones who generate the problems. What helps is a complete export including everything you might sell, your original images at the highest resolution available, your price list, your SKUs, weights and dimensions, and your intended category structure. Where something simply does not exist yet, say so, because that becomes a separately scoped piece of work instead of a surprise in week two.

What if our product data is messy or incomplete?+

Messy data is the normal case rather than the exception, and it is the strongest reason to use a service instead of a raw import tool. Supplier PDFs, distributor portals with no export, photographed print catalogues, inconsistent category names, missing weights and products with no images are all routine, and each needs a decision rather than a default. The work splits naturally: clean, consistent batches get imported, while complex, incomplete or judgement-heavy products are built by hand with written copy. We will tell you in advance which of your products falls into which group, and what the missing information is worth supplying.

Are product uploads part of broader web development services?+

Yes. When we take on the web development work as well, we run catalogue setup and product uploads from the same team, so the data structures, the storefront and the CMS are designed around one set of rules instead of being reconciled afterwards. Uploads are still scoped and quoted on their own, but you get a single partner accountable for the whole path from supplier file to live product page.

Do you handle uploads and store development for London or USA clients?+

Yes. Delivery is fully remote, so clients across London and the wider UK, the USA, Canada, Australia, New Zealand and the UAE work with the same team and the same process as everyone else. If you are weighing web development agencies in London against web development agencies in the USA, the rate card and the checkpoints are the same in both. There is no regional rate card and no travel component, and both upload-only projects and build-plus-upload projects are quoted as a fixed scope before any work starts.

How is web development cost involved if I only need uploads?+

Uploads are priced separately, so a catalogue-only project carries no development charge at all. If the store does need development work alongside, that web development cost is shown as its own line rather than buried in the per-product rate, and both parts appear in one fixed-scope quote. As a rough and hedged guide, comparable custom store builds typically run into the thousands of USD for a small site, but the real number is set by templates, integrations, page count and content, so ask for the itemised figure rather than a general estimate.
