What Is SEO? How Search Engines Rank Pages in 2026

🗓️ Sep 25, 2026⏱️ 29 min read✍️ KorTechX Team
KT
Written by the KorTechX Engineering Team
Engineers and SEO specialists who build hand-coded, high-performance websites, apps and AI automation for growing businesses across the US, Canada, UK, Australia, New Zealand and the UAE.

SEO — search engine optimisation — is the practice of making your web pages findable, understandable, and rankable by search engines so that people who are actively looking for what you offer can actually find you. At its core, search engines do three things: they decide what each query means, determine which pages best match that meaning, and assess how trustworthy and useful each page is compared to every other candidate. Everything in this article flows from that simple mental model.

Key takeaways

  • Search engines work by crawling, indexing, and then ranking pages against hundreds of signals organised around relevance, authority, and user experience.
  • Understanding search intent — whether someone is looking to go somewhere, learn something, or buy something — is the single most important strategic skill in SEO.
  • On-page SEO (content, titles, headings, internal links), technical SEO (site speed, mobile-friendliness, structured data), and off-page authority (links, mentions, reputation) all work together; neglecting any one weakens the others.
  • AI Overviews and AI-driven search features are changing how results appear, but the fundamentals — answering real questions with trustworthy, well-structured content — matter more than ever.
  • SEO is a long-term investment. Technical improvements can show results in weeks, but competitive keyword rankings typically take months of consistent effort.

How search engines actually work

To understand SEO, you first need a clear picture of what search engines are doing behind the scenes. The process has three major stages: crawling, indexing, and ranking.

Crawling: finding pages on the web

Search engines use automated programs — often called crawlers, bots, or spiders — that follow links from one page to the next across the entire public internet. Googlebot, for example, starts with a list of known URLs, fetches the content, and then discovers new pages by following every hyperlink it finds. If no crawler can reach a page — because it is blocked, behind a login, or simply has no links pointing to it — that page is invisible to search engines regardless of how good it is.

You can influence crawling in two practical ways. First, robots.txt is a plain-text file on your site that tells crawlers which pages or directories they should skip. It is useful for keeping crawlers out of admin areas or duplicate test pages, but it is easy to accidentally block important content, so it requires care. Second, an XML sitemap is a structured list of the pages you want search engines to discover. It does not guarantee indexing, but it helps crawlers prioritise and find pages faster — especially on large or newly launched websites where internal linking may not yet be comprehensive.

Indexing: storing and organising content

Once a crawler fetches a page, the search engine processes it, extracts the text, images, and structured data, and stores a processed version in a massive index — essentially a colossal database. This index is what search engines actually query when someone types a search. Not every crawled page makes it into the index. Pages that are thin on content, duplicated extensively across the web, or marked with a noindex tag are often excluded. Search engines also look at canonical tags, which tell them which version of a page is the authoritative one when duplicate or very similar content exists at multiple URLs. If you sell red shoes and the same product appears at three different URLs because of sorting parameters, a canonical tag helps the search engine consolidate signals to a single URL rather than diluting them.

Ranking: deciding the order of results

When someone searches, the engine runs its index against an algorithm that weighs hundreds of ranking signals. No single signal is a magic bullet; rankings emerge from the combined strength of many factors. At the highest level, though, these signals cluster into three families — relevance, authority and trust, and user experience — which we will cover in detail shortly. It is worth emphasising that ranking is dynamic. The moment someone else publishes a better page, or user behaviour shifts, or your content goes stale, positions can change. SEO is not a one-time setup; it is ongoing maintenance and improvement.

What a query really means: the role of search intent

The words someone types into a search box are just the surface. Behind every query is an intent — a goal the searcher wants to accomplish. Search engines have become remarkably sophisticated at inferring that intent, and understanding it is arguably the most important strategic element of SEO.

Navigational intent means the searcher already knows what they want and is trying to reach a specific page or brand. Queries like 'Gmail login' or 'Apple support' are navigational. It is rarely practical for a small business to compete for another brand's navigational terms, but ensuring your own brand's navigational queries lead directly to the right pages on your site is essential. You can check which navigational queries bring visitors to your site in Google Search Console and use that insight to make sure the landing pages match expectation.

Informational intent means the searcher wants to learn something. 'How does solar energy work' or 'what is a franchise agreement' are informational queries. These are enormous opportunities for businesses because they allow you to demonstrate expertise and build trust before a purchase decision is made. Content targeting informational intent should be genuinely helpful — thorough, clear, and structured so that the reader does not need to go back and search again.

Transactional intent means the searcher is ready to act — to buy, sign up, book, or download. 'Buy running shoes online' or 'emergency plumber near me' signal transactional or commercial intent. Pages targeting these queries should make the next step frictionless, with clear calls to action, pricing information, and trust signals like reviews.

Many queries fall into a grey zone known as investigational or commercial investigation — someone is comparing options but not yet ready to buy. 'Best CRM for small business' or 'Joomla vs WordPress 2026' are investigational queries. Ranking for these terms means providing genuinely balanced, expert comparisons rather than thinly disguised sales pages. Investigational searchers typically visit multiple pages, read reviews, and weigh options before they commit, so the businesses that win their trust during the research phase capture them at the moment of decision.

The critical practical lesson is this: the same keywords can serve different intents depending on context. Before writing any page, ask yourself what the searcher actually wants at that stage. If you get the intent wrong — say, serving a hard sales page when the searcher wants education — you will struggle to rank no matter how many links you build.

The ranking signals: relevance, authority, and user experience

Modern search algorithms consider an enormous range of factors, but they can be grouped into three broad signal families. Understanding how they interact gives you a framework for every SEO decision you will make.

Relevance: does this page match what the searcher wants?

Relevance is the most intuitive signal family. Search engines examine whether the content of a page addresses the topic and intent behind a query. They look at the words on the page, the headings, the URL, the anchor text of links pointing to it, and increasingly, the semantic relationships between concepts. This is why keyword research still matters — but keyword stuffing is counterproductive. Modern search engines understand topic clusters and contextual meaning far beyond simple keyword matching.

This also connects to Google's helpful content system, which evaluates whether content is primarily written for people or primarily engineered for search engines. Content that reads like it was generated to fill a keyword gap, without genuine insight or originality, is increasingly penalised. The antidote is straightforward: write for a real person who has a real problem, and include the knowledge, nuance, and specific detail that only someone with actual experience in the field would provide.

Authority and trust: is this page credible?

Authority is largely built through links from other websites, but it extends to brand mentions, citations, and the overall reputation of the domain. When a reputable, relevant site links to yours, it functions something like a vote of confidence. Not all links are equal — a single link from a well-known industry publication carries far more weight than hundreds of links from obscure directories.

Google's E-E-A-T framework — Experience, Expertise, Authoritativeness, and Trustworthiness — is not a direct ranking factor in the way page speed is, but it is the conceptual lens through which quality raters evaluate search results, and the signals that feed into E-E-A-T clearly influence rankings. A page about medical advice written by a named doctor with verifiable credentials on a well-established health site will outperform the same topic written anonymously on a brand-new blog, all else being equal. For your business, this means author bios, real credentials, case studies, and original research are not vanity — they are strategic assets.

Trust also encompasses technical signals. An HTTPS site with clear privacy policies, visible contact information, and transparent ownership is more trustworthy than an anonymous site with no such signals. These are table stakes, not luxuries.

User experience and engagement: do people find this page useful?

Search engines increasingly measure how users interact with results. If a large percentage of searchers click your result and then immediately return to the search results — a pattern called pogo-sticking — it signals that your page did not satisfy the query. Conversely, long dwell times and low bounce rates suggest the content met expectations.

Google's Core Web Vitals are explicit, measurable user experience metrics: Largest Contentful Paint (LCP) measures how quickly the main content of a page loads; Interaction to Next Paint (INP) measures how responsive a page is to user interactions; and Cumulative Layout Shift (CLS) measures visual stability — whether elements jump around as the page loads. These are direct ranking signals and, more importantly, they directly affect whether a visitor stays or leaves.

Freshness: keeping up with a changing world

Relevance is not a snapshot — it is a moving target. Search engines favour pages that reflect the current state of the world and the current state of best practice. This is immediately obvious for time-sensitive topics like events, product releases, or regulatory changes, where the most recent information is the most valuable. But freshness also matters for evergreen topics in more subtle ways. An article about financing a business, tax rules, or software recommendations written five years ago contains a mixture of still-useful principles and increasingly outdated details. Search engines do not automatically discard old content, but they do weigh signals — publication and update dates, the consistency and breadth of new information across the web, and the behaviour of searchers — to decide whether a newer answer should be preferred. The practical lesson is to keep your highest-value content genuinely current. Schedule regular reviews of your key pages, update facts, refresh examples, and add genuinely new information rather than simply changing a date.

A well-optimised page on a poorly structured site will underperform relative to its potential.

Where keyword research fits in

All of this on-page work starts with a research question: which topics and phrases represent genuine demand for your business? Keyword research is the practice of identifying the language your potential customers actually use when they search — which is frequently different from how you would describe your own services. A lawyer who writes 'legal representation' will miss the person searching 'how to fight a wrongful termination'. The goal is to build a map of queries that correspond, at the right stage of intent, to what your business actually offers.

Practical keyword research in 2026 involves a blend of tools and judgement. Keyword research tools suggest volumes, competition levels, and related terms; Google's autocomplete and 'people also ask' boxes reveal real user phrasing; and your own customer conversations, sales enquiries, and website analytics reveal the vocabulary your audience already uses. The output is not a list of phrases to sprinkle into pages so much as a content plan: which pages exist, which queries each page should target, and which new pages the business genuinely needs. A single service page can rank for dozens — sometimes hundreds — of closely related query variations, which is why intent mapping matters more than chasing individual keywords one at a time.

Writing for people, structuring for machines

Modern search ranking is a two-audience problem. Your content must genuinely serve the human reader — answer the question, cover the necessary nuance, and leave them satisfied — while simultaneously making that value machine-readable through clear headings, natural keyword placement, and sensible structure. These two goals only seem to conflict. Content that is genuinely clear, well-organised, and specific for human readers is usually the same content that search engines can understand best. The warning signs to avoid are the reverse: keyword stuffing, unnatural phrasing, repeated headings that say the same thing, and content that is padded with generic prose to reach a length. Build around genuine value and structure it well, and you will satisfy both audiences at once.

Technical SEO: the infrastructure underneath

Technical SEO ensures that search engines can efficiently find, crawl, render, and index your site. It is less visible than content but foundational — without a technically sound site, even excellent content struggles to rank.

Crawl budget and site architecture

Crawl budget is the number of pages a search engine will crawl on your site within a given time frame. For small business websites with a few dozen or even a few hundred pages, crawl budget is rarely a constraint. It becomes a concern for large e-commerce sites with thousands of product pages or sites with many thin, low-value URLs generated by filters and parameters. Good site architecture — a logical hierarchy with clean internal linking — helps crawlers spend their time on your most important pages.

Site speed and Core Web Vitals

Page speed is both a direct ranking signal and a critical factor in whether visitors stay on your page. Core Web Vitals — LCP, INP, and CLS — provide standardised, measurable benchmarks. For LCP, aim for under 2.5 seconds. For INP, aim for under 200 milliseconds. For CLS, aim for under 0.1. Achieving these targets often involves image optimisation, efficient code, fast hosting, and careful use of third-party scripts. The connection between speed, bounce rates, and revenue makes this one of the highest-ROI technical improvements you can make. We explore this in detail in our article on why slow sites lose customers.

Mobile-friendliness

Google uses mobile-first indexing, meaning it primarily evaluates the mobile version of your site for ranking purposes. A responsive design that works well on phones and tablets is not optional — it is the baseline expectation. Test your pages on actual devices, not just desktop emulators. Touch targets, font sizes, and horizontal scrolling all matter.

Structured data

Structured data — typically implemented as JSON-LD — provides explicit metadata about your content. It tells search engines that a particular string of text is a phone number, a review rating, a product price, or an event date. Properly implemented structured data can produce rich results — enhanced listings with star ratings, FAQ dropdowns, or business hours — that significantly increase visibility and click-through rate.

HTTPS and security

HTTPS is a confirmed ranking signal, but more importantly, it is a trust signal. Browsers actively warn users when they are on unencrypted HTTP pages. For any site handling user data — which includes contact forms and analytics — HTTPS is non-negotiable.

Canonical tags and duplicate content

If the same or very similar content exists at multiple URLs — common in e-commerce with filtered category pages — canonical tags consolidate ranking signals to the preferred URL. Without them, search engines may index the wrong version or split ranking signals across duplicates, weakening all versions.

Diagnosing technical problems the right way

Technical SEO issues are rarely visible on the surface of a website, which is exactly why they are easy to miss. The right tooling makes them visible. Google Search Console shows you how Google crawls your site, which pages are indexed and which are not, whether there are mobile usability problems, and whether Core Web Vitals thresholds are being met in the field data Google actually collects. A full site crawl performed with specialised software goes deeper, surfacing orphaned pages with no internal links, pages that are overly deep in the architecture, duplicate title tags, and dozens of other problems that compound quietly over time. If you are DIY-ing your SEO, commit to a quarterly check: run a crawl, review Search Console for increases in crawl errors or indexation drops, and test your key pages with PageSpeed Insights. Most technical problems announce themselves in these tools long before they become visible as traffic declines.

Off-page SEO: building authority beyond your website

On-page and technical SEO get your site in the running. Off-page SEO — primarily link building and brand reputation — determines whether you outperform competitors for competitive terms.

Links: the original ranking signal

Links from other websites remain one of the strongest ranking signals. Each quality link from a relevant, reputable site acts as a vote of confidence, passing a portion of that site's authority to yours. The key word is quality. A handful of links from respected industry sources, news outlets, or authoritative blogs will move the needle far more than hundreds of links from low-quality directories or link farms.

Effective link building in 2026 is overwhelmingly a function of earning attention rather than manufacturing links. The most sustainable strategies include:

  • Digital PR and original research: Publishing data, surveys, or unique insights that journalists and bloggers want to reference.
  • Expert contributions: Guest articles, podcast interviews, and speaking engagements that naturally generate links and brand mentions.
  • Creating genuinely link-worthy content: Tools, calculators, comprehensive guides, or original frameworks that other sites reference as a resource.
  • Building real relationships: Partnerships, sponsorships, and community involvement that result in organic mentions and links.

What does not work — and risks penalties — is buying links, participating in link exchanges at scale, or using automated tools to generate links on spammy sites. Search engines are increasingly effective at identifying and devaluing manipulative link schemes. The long-term cost of a penalty far outweighs any short-term gain.

Brand mentions and entity signals

Even without a direct link, mentions of your business name across the web contribute to your entity's authority in search engines' knowledge graphs. Consistent NAP (name, address, phone number) across directories, social profiles, and industry listings strengthens your local and brand signals.

Local SEO: its own discipline

If your business serves customers in a specific geographic area — a restaurant, dental practice, law firm, or home services company — local SEO deserves dedicated attention because it operates somewhat differently from traditional organic SEO.

Google Business Profile

Your Google Business Profile (GBP) is the single most important local SEO asset. It determines how your business appears in Google Maps, the local pack (the map and three listings that appear at the top of local searches), and in Knowledge Panels. Claiming, verifying, and fully completing your profile — accurate categories, business hours, service areas, photos, and regular posts — is foundational. Neglecting your GBP is like having an unlisted phone number.

Reviews and reputation

Reviews influence both ranking and conversion. The volume, velocity, and sentiment of reviews are factors in local ranking algorithms. More importantly, a business with 50 genuine four-and-a-half-star reviews is far more compelling to a searcher than one with three reviews and no recent activity. Actively encouraging satisfied customers to leave honest reviews — and responding to all reviews, positive and negative — is one of the highest-leverage local SEO activities.

Citations and local links

Citations are mentions of your business name, address, and phone number across online directories and industry listings. Consistency matters — variations in your name or address across directories create confusion for both search engines and customers. Building citations on relevant, high-quality directories while cleaning up inconsistencies is a practical, ongoing task. Local links — from chambers of commerce, local news outlets, community organisations, and industry associations — carry particular weight for local ranking.

For businesses with physical locations or defined service areas, local SEO is not optional — it is where the vast majority of high-intent local queries convert. We have a deeper exploration of this topic in our guide on how to rank on Google Maps in 2026.

AI-era search: how AI Overviews are changing the landscape

The search landscape in 2026 looks significantly different from even two years ago. AI Overviews — summaries generated by artificial intelligence that appear at the top of search results — are now a common feature across many query types. Understanding how they work is essential for any business investing in search visibility.

How AI Overviews select and cite sources

AI Overviews synthesise information from multiple sources, then present a summary that often includes citations or links to the pages it drew from. The selection algorithm prioritises pages that provide clear, factual, well-structured information that can be reliably extracted and attributed. Content that is vague, poorly structured, or lacks clear sourcing is less likely to be cited.

This has a direct implication for content strategy: answer-first content that leads with a clear, direct response before elaborating is far more likely to be picked up by AI Overviews. Structured facts — specific data points, clear definitions, step-by-step processes, and well-organised comparisons — are easier for AI systems to extract and attribute.

What this means for your content strategy

The rise of AI Overviews does not make SEO less important — it changes what great SEO content looks like. Pages that are genuinely authoritative, well-structured, and written with clear expertise are the ones AI systems cite. Pages that exist primarily to capture traffic without offering real value are the ones being bypassed.

Practically, this means:

  • Lead with the answer, then provide depth — do not bury the key information under paragraphs of preamble.
  • Use clear, factual claims with supporting evidence and sources.
  • Structure content with descriptive headings so that specific sections can be extracted and cited.
  • Include original data, specific examples, and expert perspective that cannot be found on competing pages.
  • Make your expertise and authorship visible — AI systems increasingly weigh source credibility.

The businesses that treat AI search as an evolution of SEO rather than a replacement for it will find themselves ahead. The fundamentals of creating genuinely useful, well-structured, authoritative content have never been more relevant.

Entity-based understanding and the knowledge graph

Beneath the surface of both traditional results and AI Overviews sits a powerful organising idea: search engines increasingly reason about entities — distinct, well-defined things like businesses, people, places, products, and concepts — and the relationships between them. Rather than merely matching strings of text, modern systems attempt to resolve queries to entities. A search for your company's name should be understood as a query about the entity your company, pulling together information from your site, your profiles, directory listings, and references across the web into a coherent understanding.

The practical implications are subtle but significant. Consistency of your identity across the web — the same business name, the same address format, the same categories, the same brand slug on your social profiles — strengthens the entity signals search engines can collect about you. Structured data makes your identity explicit and machine-readable. Earning references across reputable, relevant websites reinforces the picture. In an era of AI-heavy search, that coherent, well-evidenced entity profile is exactly the foundation an AI system needs to feel confident citing you as the authoritative answer to a question.

One of the most common questions — and one where honesty matters more than optimism. SEO timeframes depend heavily on your starting point, your competition, and the aggressiveness of your efforts. Here is a general framework:

SEO is a compounding investment — the results build over time, and the cumulative effect of consistent, high-quality work is substantial. But patience is part of the deal.

DIY SEO vs hiring an agency

Many small business owners start with do-it-yourself SEO — and there is a great deal you can accomplish on your own, especially in the early stages. The question is not whether DIY SEO works; it is where DIY reaches its limits and where professional expertise delivers disproportionate value.

What you can do yourself

  • Claim and fully optimise your Google Business Profile. This is free, straightforward, and high-impact for local businesses.
  • Write genuinely helpful content. If you are an expert in your field — and you are — you can create content that reflects real experience. Your perspective and specificity are assets that agencies cannot manufacture.
  • Fix obvious technical issues. Basic site speed improvements (compressing images, removing unused plugins, upgrading hosting), adding HTTPS, and fixing broken links are within reach for most business owners with basic web skills.
  • Encourage and manage reviews. Ask satisfied customers for reviews. Respond to them thoughtfully. This is a habit, not a skill gap.
  • Build internal links. As you publish content, link related pages to each other. This is free and improves both user experience and crawlability.

Where specialists earn their fee

  • Technical SEO auditing. Identifying crawl issues, render problems, indexation gaps, and site architecture weaknesses requires specialised tools and experience. What looks fine on the surface can hide issues that suppress performance across the entire site.
  • Strategic keyword research and competitive analysis. Understanding which terms are worth pursuing, which are too competitive, and where the gaps are is a discipline in itself. An experienced strategist saves you from wasting months on terms that will never produce results.
  • Link building. Earning high-quality links is labour-intensive, relationship-driven, and easy to do poorly. Professional link building campaigns draw on existing relationships, media contacts, and outreach experience that takes years to develop.
  • Content strategy at scale. Knowing which topics to address, in what order, with what depth, and how they interconnect into a coherent content architecture is a strategic function that compounds the value of every individual piece of content.
  • Analytics, attribution, and ROI measurement. Connecting SEO activity to business outcomes — revenue, leads, customer acquisition cost — requires analytical rigour and the ability to interpret data in context.
  • AI search optimisation. Adapting content and technical implementation for AI Overviews, structured data requirements, and evolving search features is an emerging discipline where early expertise provides a measurable advantage.

The honest answer for most small businesses is a hybrid approach. Handle what you can handle well — GBP, reviews, basic content, basic technical hygiene — and invest in professional support for the areas where expertise translates directly into revenue. A good agency does not replace your involvement; it amplifies the expertise you already have. Whether you are starting from scratch or have been managing SEO yourself for a while, it is worth having a specialist audit your site at least once — the perspective almost always uncovers issues you cannot see from the inside. 90-day starter checklist for small business owners

If you are starting from scratch, the volume of SEO advice can be paralysing. Here are the ten highest-leverage actions to prioritise in your first 90 days:

  • Verify Google Search Console and submit your sitemap. Search Console is free and gives you direct insight into how Google sees your site — what it indexes, what errors exist, and what queries bring traffic. Submitting your XML sitemap ensures crawlers discover your pages efficiently.
  • Claim and fully complete your Google Business Profile. If you serve local customers, this is the single highest-ROI action you can take. Add all categories, hours, photos, service areas, and a compelling business description.
  • Audit and rewrite your title tags and meta descriptions. Every page on your site should have a unique, descriptive title tag that includes your focus keyword naturally and a compelling meta description that entices clicks. This is often the quickest on-page win.
  • Map keyword intent to every important page. For each page, write down: what query is this page targeting, and what is the searcher's intent? If you cannot answer both clearly, the page needs rethinking.
  • Fix your internal linking structure. Ensure every important page is reachable within three clicks from the homepage. Add contextual links from blog posts to service pages and between related content. Use descriptive anchor text.
  • Run a site speed pass. Test your site with Google PageSpeed Insights. Address the most impactful issues: compress and properly size images, remove unnecessary plugins or scripts, and evaluate your hosting. Even modest speed improvements can have a measurable effect on bounce rates.
  • Request reviews from recent satisfied customers. If you have happy customers, ask them for Google reviews. Provide a direct link to make it easy. Aim for consistent, ongoing reviews rather than a one-time burst.
  • Fix critical technical errors. Use Search Console to identify crawl errors, broken links, 404 pages, and mobile usability issues. Address the most impactful ones first — a broken page is a page that cannot rank.
  • Publish your first two or three pieces of cornerstone content. Write genuinely helpful articles that address the most common questions your customers ask. Focus on quality over quantity — two excellent articles outperform ten thin ones.
  • Set up basic analytics. Connect Google Analytics 4 and ensure conversion tracking is in place so you can measure what matters. SEO without measurement is just activity.

These actions are not everything, but they are the right starting points. They build a foundation that makes everything else you do more effective.

The bottom line

SEO in 2026 is more complex than it was five years ago, but the fundamentals remain remarkably stable. Search engines are trying to connect real people with the best possible answers to their questions. If your pages provide genuinely useful, well-structured, trustworthy answers — and if your technical infrastructure allows search engines to find, understand, and present those pages — you will earn visibility. The specifics of how algorithms work will continue to evolve, especially as AI reshapes the search experience, but the businesses that invest in creating genuine value will continue to be rewarded.

The most important thing to understand is that SEO is not a trick or a hack. It is the long-term practice of making your business findable by the people who need what you offer. It requires patience, consistency, and a willingness to improve iteratively. The businesses that approach it that way — with realistic expectations and a commitment to genuine quality — are the ones that build durable, compounding visibility over time.

Frequently asked questions

What is SEO in simple terms?+

SEO — search engine optimisation — is the process of making your website pages findable and rankable in search engine results. It involves helping search engines understand what your pages are about, confirming that your content is trustworthy and useful, and ensuring the technical foundation of your site allows crawlers to access and index your content efficiently.

How does Google decide which pages to rank first?+

Google evaluates pages based on hundreds of signals that broadly cluster into relevance (does the content match what the searcher wants), authority and trust (is the page and its source credible), and user experience (do people find the page genuinely useful). No single signal determines ranking; results emerge from the combined strength of many factors working together.

How long does it take for SEO to work?+

It depends on the activity and the competition. Technical fixes can show results in two to eight weeks. On-page changes to existing content typically take four to twelve weeks. New content targeting less competitive keywords may rank within two to four months. Competitive, high-volume keywords can take six to eighteen months or longer. SEO is a long-term investment, not an overnight fix.

Can I do SEO myself or do I need an agency?+

You can accomplish a great deal on your own — especially in the early stages with Google Business Profile optimisation, basic content creation, review management, and simple technical fixes. Professional SEO expertise becomes valuable when you need strategic keyword research, technical auditing, link building, content strategy at scale, or advanced analytics. Many businesses benefit from a hybrid approach, handling what they can internally and investing in specialist support where expertise directly impacts results.

How has AI changed SEO in 2026?+

AI Overviews now appear at the top of many search results, synthesising information from multiple sources and presenting summaries with citations. This does not make SEO less important — it changes what good SEO content looks like. Content that is well-structured, factually clear, and authored by credible sources is more likely to be cited by AI systems. Answer-first content, structured data, and visible expertise are increasingly important.
